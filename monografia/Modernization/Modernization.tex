\chapter{Experiment Modernization}
\label{chap:modernization}

The main experiment of the previous chapters runs part of its generators in \codename{R} and \codename{Java}, as \cref{chap:methodology_reassessment} describes, and its protocol carries the constraints of that environment. Its hyperparameter search penalizes the execution time and stops at a time limit, and its datasets have at most 10000 samples, because some of the models are too slow for an unrestricted search, as seen in \cref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}. We set the ranges of that search by hand, as values that seemed reasonable after a brief survey of the literature \cite{cao2006density,aggarwal2003framework,hyde2017fully,ackermann2012streamkmpp,bezerra2016tedacloud,bezerra2020autocloud}. In \cref{sec:mi_selection}, we found that the objective of the search observes too short a segment of the stream to recognize the configurations that are best in the long run, and that the search is itself a source of variation.

In this chapter, we replace the environment, the benchmark models and the hyperparameter selection, and repeat the main experiment under them. We evaluate \MicroTEDAClusPlus as \cref{sec:mi_final_model} defines it, without changes.

\section{Environment}
\label{sec:mod_environment}

We write the experiment in \codename{Python} only. Its numerical parts run in compiled code through the libraries that provide them, and no other runtime is involved. \MicroTEDAClusPlus keeps the implementation of \cref{sec:mi_final_model}, and we use every model through one interface: it predicts the labels of a batch, learns the batch, and reports the number of structures of its summary of the stream, which generalizes the microcluster count of \cref{sec:evaluation_metrics}. We label as noise a sample that a model predicts before it has any cluster.

\subsection{Stream Generators}
\label{subsec:mod_generators}

Four generators of \cref{sec:datasets} ran in \codename{R}: that of \Cassini and those of \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo and \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, the last from MOA \cite{bifet2010moa} through \codename{streamMOA} \cite{streamMOA2024}. We port them to \codename{Python}, following their reference implementations step by step. The Chameleon datasets remain the files of \cref{sec:datasets}, and the scalability streams were already generated in \codename{Python}. A stream is determined by its dataset and its run, which seed its generator.

We check each port against the streams that \codename{R} generated for the previous experiment through the ARI of three models with fixed hyperparameters (\cref{tab:mod_generators_behaviour}), compared by the Mann-Whitney test with the Holm correction and the threshold on Cliff's delta of \cref{sec:statistical_significance}. No test finds a difference.

The interface through which the previous experiment generated \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents gives the noise the class value of the first cluster that the generator creates after the initial ones, so the samples of that cluster are labeled as noise. The port labels the noise apart from every cluster, and in the comparison above we apply the labeling of the reference to both sources. \Cref{tab:mod_generators_rbf_noise} shows the share of samples labeled as noise along the stream under each labeling.

\subsection{Length and Order of the Streams}
\label{subsec:mod_streams}

The generated streams are five times longer than those of the previous experiment, and so are their tuning segments, in answer to \cref{sec:mi_selection}. The Chameleon files keep their size. We shuffle the streams without concept drift, which are those of \Cassini, of the Chameleon datasets and of the scalability datasets, anew in every run. Each run of a Chameleon dataset thus has a stream of its own, where in the previous experiment all its runs processed the same stream (\cref{subsec:mi_selection_consequences}). The concept drift streams keep their order, which carries the drift.

\section{Benchmark Models}
\label{sec:mod_models}

We choose the benchmark models by three criteria:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item They are established in the literature of stream clustering;
    \item Their implementations are maintained libraries that the experiment imports instead of copying;
    \item They are fast enough to be tuned without a time limit.
\end{enumerate}

We retain five models: \DenStream \cite{cao2006density} and \DBSTREAM \cite{hahsler2016dbstream}, from \codename{River} \cite{montiel2021river}; \BIRCH \cite{zhang1996birch} and \MiniBatchKMeans \cite{sculley2010webscale}, from \codename{scikit-learn} \cite{pedregosa2011scikit}; and \BICO \cite{fichtenberger2013bico}, from the \codename{bico} package. They summarize the stream by density-based microclusters that fade, \DenStream and \DBSTREAM; by a tree of cluster features, \BIRCH; by a coreset, \BICO; and by centroids, \MiniBatchKMeans. This set replaces that of \cref{sec:benchmark_models}.

\Cref{tab:mod_hyperparameters} lists the hyperparameters that the search selects for each model. We search a parameter if it is part of the clustering method, and keep the default of its library if it only sets a unit of time or the shape of a data structure. \DenStream and \DBSTREAM forget at a rate that we search as its half-life, in samples. We search the outlier threshold $\beta\mu$ of \DenStream, which its formulation requires to exceed one, as its position between one and $\mu$.

\section{Hyperparameter Selection}
\label{sec:mod_selection}

The search remains the Bayesian optimization of \cref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning} on the first 20\% of each stream, and we evaluate the selected model prequentially on the rest. Three things change: where the ranges come from, what the objective measures, and what bounds the search.

\subsection{Ranges by Rule}
\label{subsec:mod_ranges}

Each hyperparameter belongs to a category, given in \cref{tab:mod_hyperparameters}, and each category has one rule that computes its range from the tuning segment, given in \cref{tab:mod_rules}. A rule uses only what a deployment of the model also has before the stream continues: the samples of the segment and their labels. Below the median distance from a sample to its nearest neighbor, a structure of that radius holds a single sample, and above the median distance between two samples it holds half of the segment, so the distances of the rules span the scales at which a structure can summarize the data. The longest horizon is the length of the segment, the longest that the segment can tell apart from not forgetting at all. The rules replace ranges set by hand, which \cref{tab:mi_search_range_selection} showed to depend on the scale of the data. \Cref{tab:mod_ranges} gives the ranges that they produce in each dataset, and \cref{sec:real_protocol} widens the rules for the real datasets.

\subsection{Objective and Search}
\label{subsec:mod_objective}

The objective is the median of the batch ARI over the second half of the tuning segment. It is the quantity that the Overall ARI reports, computed after the configuration has had the first half of the segment to form its summary, which addresses the slow maturation shown in \cref{tab:mi_selection_maturation}. It has no term of execution time.

Every model receives 100 trials, without a time limit. The search of a task is seeded by its dataset, its model and its run, and its objective involves no measurement of time, so the selection depends neither on the speed of the machine nor on its load. To measure what the change of protocol does, we also tune \MicroTEDAClusPlus by the previous protocol of \cref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}, with its previous ranges, on the same streams.

\section{Execution and Measurements}
\label{sec:mod_measurements}

A task is a dataset, a model and a run, and the experiment executes the tasks of 94 runs, 13 datasets and the six models in parallel processes, which give the quality of the models and the size of their summaries. Parallel execution disturbs the execution time, under which the main experiment of the previous chapters timed its tasks. We therefore measure time in a separate pass, by the procedure of \cref{subsec:mi_optimization_time}: we execute the tasks of the first ten runs again, one at a time, with their selected hyperparameters. A new model of the task first processes the first three batches of the evaluation segment and is discarded, so that what is created on first use is in place, and we then time another new model over the evaluation segment once. A single execution replaces the minimum of three, since the three executions of a task agree closely (\cref{tab:mi_validation_serial_cost}).

We measure memory in another pass, over the tasks of the same ten runs, by tracking every allocation of the process with \codename{memray} \cite{memray}, which counts the memory allocated by compiled code as well as that allocated by \codename{Python}. We keep two quantities: the memory still allocated when the stream ends, which is what the model holds, and the peak during the evaluation, which adds the working memory of its steps. Each task runs in a new process, so that it inherits nothing that an earlier task left allocated. A model of the task first processes the whole evaluation segment without tracking, so that what is created on first use is in place however late it is created, and we then track new models of the task three times and keep the smallest values. These quantities are not comparable with the memory reported in the previous chapters, which counted only the \codename{Python} objects of a model.

We compare \MicroTEDAClusPlus with each benchmark model by the test of \cref{sec:statistical_significance}, whose conventions the tables follow, with the abbreviations of \cref{tab:synthetic_datasets}. The comparisons of time and memory rest on ten runs instead of 94, so they separate fewer models.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:mod_results}

\subsection{Clustering Quality}
\label{subsec:mod_quality}

\input{Modernization/artifacts/experiment/quality.tex}

\MicroTEDAClusPlus is better than every benchmark model in the Overall ARI of \BenchmarkOne, and than all but \BIRCH in that of \Cassini (\cref{tab:mod_quality}). It is worse than all of them in \BenchmarkTwo, on both metrics, and at the penultimate batch of \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents. In the Chameleon datasets, it is indistinguishable from most of them, worse than \BICO in \ChameleonDSThree and better than \DenStream and \DBSTREAM in the Overall ARI of \ChameleonDSFour. In \cref{subsec:mi_main_quality}, no benchmark model was better than \MicroTEDAClusPlus in the streams with concept drift. The benchmark models, the streams and the protocol all differ here, and the protocol alone changes its results in \BenchmarkTwo little (\cref{tab:mod_protocols}).

In the scalability streams, no benchmark model is better than \MicroTEDAClusPlus from two dimensions on, and every one is worse in most of these streams (\cref{tab:mod_scalability,fig:mod_scalability_quality}). Along the streams of \cref{fig:mod_scalability_quality}, \DenStream scores zero over the first several hundred samples or more: it labels every sample as noise until its initialization buffer is full, and the search selects long buffers. The objective scores only the second half of the tuning segment, where a late start costs nothing, while the Overall ARI counts the whole stream.

\subsection{The Change of Protocol}
\label{subsec:mod_protocols}

\input{Modernization/artifacts/experiment/protocols.tex}

\Cref{tab:mod_protocols} compares \MicroTEDAClusPlus under the two protocols on the same streams. The protocol by rules raises its Overall ARI in six datasets and lowers it in \BenchmarkTwo, and raises its Steady-State ARI in five datasets without lowering it in any. The largest differences are at the penultimate batch of \BenchmarkOne and \Cassini. This agrees with \cref{subsec:mi_selection_consequences}, which located the most direct improvement of the model in the selection of its hyperparameters rather than in its algorithm.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{subsec:mod_cost}

\input{Modernization/artifacts/experiment/cost.tex}

\MicroTEDAClusPlus is faster than every benchmark model in the prediction stage of four datasets, and than three of the five in the other two (\cref{tab:mod_cost}). It has no such advantage in the learning stage, where it is slower than \BICO in five datasets and faster than \BIRCH in four. \MiniBatchKMeans, which keeps only its centroids, holds less memory and reaches a lower peak than \MicroTEDAClusPlus in every dataset. Against the other benchmark models, the memory that \MicroTEDAClusPlus holds is smaller in some datasets and larger in others, while its peak is larger than those of most of them in \Cassini and \ChameleonDSThree, the datasets in which its summary is largest.

\input{Modernization/artifacts/experiment/summary_size.tex}

In the three static datasets, \MicroTEDAClusPlus keeps more structures than \DenStream, \DBSTREAM, \MiniBatchKMeans and \BICO (\cref{tab:mod_summary_size}). In the Chameleon datasets, whose streams have the same length in both experiments, it keeps over a hundred microclusters where the main experiment selected configurations with tens (\cref{tab:mi_comparison_microclusters}). The protocol by rules thus selects there the fine-grained configurations that \cref{sec:mi_selection} found to score better, and the Overall ARI of these datasets rises with them (\cref{tab:mod_protocols}).

The memory it holds grows with the dimension and stays above that of \MiniBatchKMeans at every dimension and below that of \BIRCH from two dimensions on.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:mod_summary}

The modernized experiment runs in \codename{Python} only, on ported generators validated against those of \codename{R} (\cref{subsec:mod_generators}), with five benchmark models imported from maintained libraries. Its hyperparameter selection takes its ranges from the tuning segment by rule and measures no time, so it depends neither on the speed nor on the load of the machine. Under it, \MicroTEDAClusPlus is better than every benchmark model in the Overall ARI of \BenchmarkOne, not worse than any in that of \Cassini, and not worse than any in the scalability streams from two dimensions on, but worse than all of them in \BenchmarkTwo and at the penultimate batch of \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents. It predicts faster than every benchmark model in most datasets, but does not learn faster. The protocol itself improves its results, most of all at the penultimate batch, with the fine-grained configurations that \cref{sec:mi_selection} anticipated (\cref{tab:mod_protocols}). Several of its ranges limited the search, most often those of the cluster counts and the distances, which only benchmark models use (\cref{tab:real_range_ends}), and \cref{sec:real_protocol} widens them for the real datasets.
