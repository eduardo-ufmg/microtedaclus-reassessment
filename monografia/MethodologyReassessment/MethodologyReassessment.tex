\chapter{Methodology Reassessment}
\label{chap:methodology_reassessment}

Maia et al.~\cite{maia2020evolvclus} report that \MicroTEDAClus performs on par with established stream clustering algorithms and adapts better than them to concept drift. In this chapter, we examine what produced those results: the implementation of the model (\cref{sec:model_implementation}), the benchmark models and how they were executed (\cref{sec:benchmark_models}), the preprocessing of the datasets (\cref{sec:datasets}) and the evaluation protocol (\cref{sec:prequential_sequential_offline_evaluation}). We then define the methodology that the rest of the thesis uses: the metrics (\cref{sec:evaluation_metrics}), the selection of hyperparameters and the number of runs (\cref{sec:tuning_and_runs}), and the statistical comparison (\cref{sec:statistical_significance}). Finally, we evaluate \MicroTEDAClus against six benchmark models under that methodology (\cref{sec:reassessment_results}).

\section{Model Implementation}
\label{sec:model_implementation}

The original work points to a public repository with the code of its experiments, \url{https://github.com/cseveriano/evolving_clustering}, which implements \MicroTEDAClus in \codename{src/models/evolving/EvolvingClustering.py}. To preserve it, we forked the repository at its last commit, \codename{50dd2b4}~\cite{eduardo2026evolvingclustering}.

This implementation is not the formulation of the paper, but it is the code that produced the published results. It shares features with the updated formulation of Galindo~\cite{galindo2021lastmile}, and it solves the outlier condition of microclusters with two samples (\cref{subsubsec:incorrect_condition_logic}), but it departs from the formulation in the decay of the microclusters, in their activation, in the eccentricity and in the membership.

\paragraph{Decay.} The constructor turns the hyperparameter \codename{decay} into \codename{fading\_factor} $= 1/\text{decay}$, the rate $\rho$ of the decay of Galindo (\cref{eq:decay_logic}). The factor acts in two places. The pruning (\cref{snippet:maia_implementation_prune_microclusters}) lowers, at every update, the vitality of the microclusters that are not active, while Galindo lowers that of the microclusters that the current sample does not update; activation is decided only when the macroclusters are built. The vitality update (\cref{snippet:maia_implementation_update_life}) rewards a microcluster when a sample falls within one standard deviation of its mean and penalizes it otherwise, a rule that appears in neither work and that neither explains.

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Pruning of the microclusters in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_prune_microclusters}]
def prune_micro_clusters(self):
    for mc in self.micro_clusters:
        if not mc["active"]:
            mc["life"] = mc["life"] - self.fading_factor
            if mc["life"] < 0:
                self.micro_clusters.remove(mc)
                if mc in self.changed_micro_clusters:
                    self.changed_micro_clusters.remove(mc)
                self.graph.remove_node(mc["id"])
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Update of the vitality of a microcluster in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_update_life}]
def update_life(self, xk, micro_cluster):
    previous_mean = micro_cluster["mean"]
    previous_var = micro_cluster["variance"]
    if previous_var > 0:
        d = EvolvingClustering.get_euclidean_distance(xk, previous_mean)
        dist = np.sqrt(np.sum(d))
        rt = np.sqrt(previous_var)
        micro_cluster["life"] = micro_cluster["life"] + (((rt - dist) / rt) * self.fading_factor)
    else:
        micro_cluster["life"] = 1
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Activation.} The paper activates the microclusters whose density is not below the mean density of their macrocluster (\cref{info:microcluster_activation_criterion,critique:use_of_unstable_density}). The implementation builds the macroclusters twice, as \cref{alg:microcluster_activation_criterion_pseudocode} describes in our reading of the code: it groups the intersecting microclusters into the components of a graph $\mathbb{G}_0$, activates microclusters within each component, removes the inactive ones to obtain a graph $\mathbb{G}_1$, and returns the components of $\mathbb{G}_1$ as the macroclusters. The second pass guards against the collapse of overlapping microclusters into a single macrocluster, which the original work anticipates. \Cref{snippet:maia_implementation_define_activations} also shows that only microclusters with more than two samples can be active, while the mean density is computed over all the microclusters of the component.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Construction of the macroclusters in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}
\label{alg:microcluster_activation_criterion_pseudocode}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the microclusters $m_i$, $i = 1, 2, \dots, n$
\Ensure the macroclusters $\mathcal{M}_j$, $j = 1, 2, \dots, m$
\State Build the graph $\mathbb{G}_0$ whose edges join the microclusters that satisfy \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition}
\State Build a macrocluster $\mathcal{M}_j$ for each connected component of $\mathbb{G}_0$
\For{each macrocluster $\mathcal{M}_j$}
    \State Activate its microclusters by \cref{critique:use_of_unstable_density}
\EndFor
\State Remove the inactive microclusters from $\mathbb{G}_0$ to obtain $\mathbb{G}_1$
\State Build a macrocluster $\mathcal{M}_j$ for each connected component of $\mathbb{G}_1$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Activation of the microclusters in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_define_activations}]
def define_activations(self):
    self.active_graph = self.graph.copy()
    for mg in self.macro_clusters:
        num_micro = len(mg)
        total_density = 0
        for i in mg:
            dens = next(item["density"] for item in self.micro_clusters if item["id"] == i)
            total_density += dens
        mean_density = total_density / num_micro
        for i in mg:
            mi = next(item for item in self.micro_clusters if item["id"] == i)
            mi["active"] = (mi["num_samples"] > 2) and (mi["density"] >= mean_density)
            if not mi["active"]:
                self.active_graph.remove_node(mi["id"])
    self.active_macro_clusters = list(nx.connected_components(self.active_graph))
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Outlier test.} \Cref{snippet:maia_implementation_is_outlier} applies \cref{eq:outlier_condition} to microclusters with three samples or more, and, below three, the test of the variance alone of \cref{eq:corrected_condition_logic_for_outliers}, which replaces the conjunction of \cref{critique:incorrect_condition_logic}.

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Outlier test in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_is_outlier}]
def is_outlier(self, s_ik, var_ik, norm_ecc):
    if s_ik < 3:
        outlier = (var_ik > self.variance_limit)
    else:
        mik_sik = 3 / (1 + math.exp(-0.007 * (s_ik - 100)))
        outlier_limit = ((mik_sik ** 2) + 1) / (2 * s_ik)
        outlier = (norm_ecc > outlier_limit)
    return outlier
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Eccentricity.} The eccentricity of \cref{snippet:maia_implementation_get_eccentricity} expands to \cref{eq:maia_implementation_eccentricity_eq}, which differs from the recursive eccentricity of \cref{eq:recursive_eccentricity} by the factor $4/d^2$ on its second term.

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Eccentricity in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_get_eccentricity}]
def get_eccentricity(x, num_samples, mean, var):
    if var == 0 and num_samples > 1:
        result = (1/num_samples)
    else:
        a = mean - x
        result = ((1 / num_samples) + ((np.linalg.norm(a) * 2 / len(a)) ** 2 / (num_samples * var)))
    return result
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{equation}
    \xi^i\left(x\right) = \frac{1}{S^i_k} + \frac{4\lVert\mu^i_k - x\rVert^2}{d^2S^i_k\left(\sigma^i_k\right)^2}
    \label{eq:maia_implementation_eccentricity_eq}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Membership.} The membership of a sample to a macrocluster (\cref{snippet:maia_implementation_calculate_membership}) weighs $1 - \zeta$ by the densities of the active microclusters, an expression that cannot be rewritten as the normalized typicality of \cref{eq:normalized_typicality}.

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Membership in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_calculate_membership}]
def calculate_membership(x, active_micro_clusters):
    total_density = 0
    for m in active_micro_clusters:
        total_density += m["density"]
    mb = 0
    for m in active_micro_clusters:
        d = m["density"]
        t = 1 - EvolvingClustering.get_normalized_eccentricity(x, m["num_samples"], m["mean"], m["variance"])
        mb += (d / total_density) * t
    return mb
\end{lstlisting}

The implementation of the updated formulation, which Galindo provided to us, is not public. It adds partial support for the cosine similarity, refactors the decay and moves the microclusters into a class of their own.

\section{Benchmark Models}
\label{sec:benchmark_models}

The original work compares \MicroTEDAClus with \DenStream~\cite{cao2006density}, \CluStream~\cite{aggarwal2003framework} and \StreamKM++~\cite{ackermann2012streamkmpp}, benchmarks common in the literature on stream clustering~\cite{hyde2017fully,akinosho2023comparative,wang2023data,rezaei2025,mutambik2024,jahn2026goingoffline}. It executes them from \codename{Python} through \codename{rpy2}, which calls the \codename{R} package \codename{streamMOA}, itself a wrapper of the \codename{Java} framework MOA~\cite{streamMOA2024}. These two layers of interface add to the time and memory that the experiment measures, so the comparison of the original work measures the interfaces together with the models, and a fair comparison requires every model to run in the environment of the evaluated one. Since \MicroTEDAClus is implemented in \codename{Python}, we use the implementations of \DenStream, \CluStream and \StreamKM in the \codename{River} library~\cite{montiel2021river}.

\subsection{Static Datasets}
\label{subsubsec:scaling}

According to the original work, its static datasets \datasetname{ST-D1} and \datasetname{ST-D2} are datasets introduced to evaluate the Chameleon algorithm~\cite{karypis1999chameleon}. Its repository holds the files \codename{chameleon-ds3-clean.csv} and \codename{chameleon-ds4-clean.csv}, while the Chameleon paper names the corresponding raw patterns, of 10{,}000 and 8{,}000 samples, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour. We use the raw files \codename{cluto-t7-10k.arff} and \codename{cluto-t8-8k.arff}, and those names. The files of the repository are cleaned of noise, downsampled, scaled and ordered by label. To measure what each of these steps does, we applied them in turn to \ChameleonDSThree, and tuned and evaluated \MicroTEDAClus on the result of each step by the protocol of this chapter (\cref{fig:mr_preprocessing_stages}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/preprocessing/stages.png}
    \caption[ARI and execution time of \MicroTEDAClus on \ChameleonDSThree after each preprocessing step]{ARI and execution time per batch of \MicroTEDAClus on \ChameleonDSThree after each preprocessing step, with the hyperparameters selected for it: medians and interquartile bands over 94 runs.}
    \label{fig:mr_preprocessing_stages}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Noise removal.} Removing the samples labeled as noise changes the ARI little. It is, however, a severe leak~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}: a sample is noise only if it belongs to no cluster, which is known after the clustering. In a stream, the model itself must decide which samples are noise, from the samples it has seen, or not handle noise at all~\cite{akinosho2023comparative,wang2023data,rezaei2025,mutambik2024,jahn2026goingoffline,zhang2022extension,zubaroglu2023emcstream}.

\paragraph{Downsampling.} Keeping a random half of the samples needs no information about them and is not a leak. It lowers the ARI slightly and the learning time by about half.

\paragraph{Scaling.} Min-max scaling reduces the execution time by an order of magnitude and moves the variance limit that the search selects from a median of about 5 to about 0.01. The model is therefore not robust to the choice of this hyperparameter, as the original work claims: the limit follows the scale of the data. The sensitivity study of \cref{sec:c_implementation} also finds the quality strongly dependent on it. Scaling is expected in any application, but the original work computes the minimum and the maximum of each feature over the whole dataset, which leaks the future of the stream.

\paragraph{Ordering.} Ordering the samples by label is the most harmful step. It leaks the labels and discards the order of arrival, and, since consecutive samples then share their label, most 100-sample batches hold a single cluster. The ARI then reaches 1 in most batches, and the learning time falls below a millisecond per batch over most of the stream (\cref{fig:mr_preprocessing_stages}).

The experiments of the original work on \datasetname{ST-D1} and \datasetname{ST-D2} therefore do not evaluate online stream clustering, and we use the raw \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour instead. The third static dataset, \Cassini, comes from the \codename{R} package \codename{mlbench} without preprocessing, but ordered by label; we shuffle it.

The streams with concept drift of the original work, \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo and \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, come from \codename{streamMOA}. Its plots suggest that their noise samples, labeled \codename{NA} by the package, were removed. We keep them, labeled $-1$.

The scalability experiments of the original work, which profile memory and time as the number of samples and the dimension grow, use synthetic streams of \codename{streamMOA} without noise. We generate them with noise.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:prequential_sequential_offline_evaluation}

The original work states that it applies prequential evaluation, in which ``each instance can be used to test before it is used for training'' (Maia et al.~\cite[Section 5.2.1]{maia2020evolvclus}). That is the standard practice for stream clustering~\cite{akinosho2023comparative,wang2023data,rezaei2025,mutambik2024,jahn2026goingoffline}, in line with Dawid's view of inference as forecasting future observations~\cite{dawid1984prequential}. Its code, however, implements no prequential evaluation.

For \datasetname{ST-D1} and \datasetname{ST-D2}, it learns the whole dataset and then predicts the same samples (\cref{snippet:original_work_static_evaluation_protocol}). Even for offline clustering, that is not standard practice~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage,arias2024improved,halkidi2001validation}, which prefers cross-validation~\cite{stone1974cross,kohavi1995study}. The evaluation of the \codename{C} reimplementation of \cref{sec:c_implementation} follows the same protocol for all of its datasets. For the other datasets, a function named \codename{prequential\_evaluation} learns each batch before predicting it (\cref{snippet:original_work_prequential_evaluation_function_steps}), the opposite of prequential evaluation.

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Evaluation of the static datasets in the original work}, label={snippet:original_work_static_evaluation_protocol}]
X = stream_df[['x', 'y']].values
y = stream_df['class'].values
evol_model = EvolvingClustering.EvolvingClustering(variance_limit=0.0006, debug=False)
evol_model.fit(X)
y_hat = evol_model.predict(X)
error = adjusted_rand_score(y, y_hat)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Excerpt of the function named \codename{prequential\_evaluation} in the original work}, label={snippet:original_work_prequential_evaluation_function_steps}]
if elapsed_time:
    time_start = time.time()
method.fit(train_data)
y_hat = method.predict(test_data)
y = labels[test_start:test_end]
\end{lstlisting}

Every experiment of this thesis uses prequential evaluation: each 100-sample batch is predicted before the model learns it, and the predicted labels are compared with the true ones.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}

The original work evaluates clustering quality by the adjusted Rand index (ARI)~\cite{hubert1985ari}, an external index that depends neither on the distribution of the data nor on the number of clusters~\cite{halkidi2001validation}. It summarizes the ARI of a run by its mean and standard deviation over the runs, which hides how the quality evolves along a stream: a curve that improves slowly, one that peaks and degrades, and a random one can share both. We summarize the ARI of a run by two metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{Overall ARI}, the median of the ARI of the batches of the evaluation segment;
    \item the \textbf{Steady-State ARI}, the ARI of the penultimate batch, which measures the quality after the stream has run its course and any concept drift has occurred. The last batch is avoided because it may be incomplete.
\end{itemize}

The original work reports the execution time only for its scalability streams, as the mean time per sample against the dimension, and the memory only for one run of \BenchmarkOne. We measure the cost of every model on every dataset:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{execution time} of the learning and of the prediction stages, each summed over the batches of the evaluation segment, from the wall clock inside the task. \Cref{subsec:mi_optimization_time} measures the time with each task executed alone;
    \item the \textbf{memory footprint}, the size of the model object after each batch, at its maximum and at the penultimate batch;
    \item the \textbf{microcluster count}, at its maximum and at the penultimate batch, a proxy for the memory that depends less on the implementation. \StreamKM keeps no microclusters and is left out of it.
\end{itemize}

For the scalability streams, we report the quality per dimension and plot the cost against the dimension.

\section{Hyperparameter Selection and Runs}
\label{sec:tuning_and_runs}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}
\label{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}

Every model has hyperparameters that can be tuned, even those whose papers use default values. So that no model is disadvantaged by its hyperparameters, every run begins with a search on the first 20\% of its stream, by Bayesian optimization~\cite{ozaki2025optunahub}, and the tuned model is evaluated on the remaining 80\%, which the search never sees~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}. The original work, in contrast, selects the parameters that maximize the score over the whole dataset. The search maximizes the objective of \cref{alg:hyperparameter_tuning_objective_function}: the ARI of a prequential execution on the tuning segment, without its first 5\% of predictions, less a penalty of $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ per second of execution.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Objective of the hyperparameter search}
\label{alg:hyperparameter_tuning_objective_function}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the tuning segment $(X_{opt}, y_{opt})$, the batch size, the model class and its search space, the current trial
\Ensure the score that the search maximizes
\State Draw the hyperparameters of the trial from the search space
\State Build the model with them
\State Execute it prequentially on the tuning segment, measuring the execution time $t_{total}$
\State Drop the first 5\% of the predictions
\State Compute the ARI $ari$ between the remaining predictions and their labels
\State \Return $ari - \lambda\, t_{total}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The search of each model ends at 100 trials or at 300 seconds, whichever comes first. The penalty and the time limit avoid configurations that buy a small gain in ARI with a large cost, and they keep the experiments feasible: some models are slow enough to make an unrestricted search impossible over the runs that the statistics require, so a slow model may complete fewer trials than a fast one. For the same reason, every dataset has at most 10{,}000 samples.

\subsection{Multi-run Statistics}
\label{subsec:multi_run_statistics}

A run generates its stream from its seed, tunes the model and evaluates it. We aggregate the runs by the median and the interquartile range, and not by the mean and the standard deviation, because the search may end in a poor local optimum and produce outlier runs, to which the median is robust.

We take $N = 1000$ runs as a stable reference, and choose the number of runs $k^*$ that approximates it at the point beyond which more runs bring diminishing returns, \cref{eq:kneedle}, which the Kneedle algorithm~\cite{satopaa2011kneedle} finds as the point of maximum distance to the secant joining the ends of the min--max normalized curve

\begin{equation}
k^* = \operatorname*{argmax}_k\frac{|(\tilde k_{\max}-\tilde k_{\min})(\tilde Y(k)-\tilde Y_{\min}) - (\tilde Y_{\max}-\tilde Y_{\min})(\tilde k(k)-\tilde k_{\min})|}{\sqrt{(\tilde k_{\max}-\tilde k_{\min})^2 + (\tilde Y_{\max}-\tilde Y_{\min})^2}},
\label{eq:kneedle}
\end{equation}

\begin{align}
\tilde k(k) &= \frac{k - k_{\min}}{k_{\max}-k_{\min}}, &
\tilde Y(k) &= \frac{\hat Y_{\mathrm{med}}(k) - \hat Y_{\mathrm{med}}(k_{\min})}{\hat Y_{\mathrm{med}}(k_{\max}) - \hat Y_{\mathrm{med}}(k_{\min})},
\end{align}

where $\hat Y_{\mathrm{med}}$ is a non-increasing isotonic regression~\cite{barlow1972statistical} of the worst-case convergence curve of the median

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
Y_{\mathrm{med}}(k) = \max_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \mathrm{NMAE}^{\mathrm{med}}_c(k), \\
\mathcal{C} = \{\datasetname{SCL-2}, \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo\} \\
\times \{\text{ARI}, \text{time}, \text{memory}, \text{microcluster count}\}.
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}

For a configuration $c \in \mathcal{C}$ and a subset size $k \in \{k_{\min},\dots,N\}$, let $S_{c,k,1},\dots,S_{c,k,B}$ be $B$ independent draws, uniform over the size-$k$ subsets of $\{1,\dots,N\}$, with subsample statistics

\begin{equation}
m_{c,k,b} = \operatorname{median}(\mathcal{D}_c[S_{c,k,b}]), \qquad
q_{c,k,b} = Q_3(\mathcal{D}_c[S_{c,k,b}]) - Q_1(\mathcal{D}_c[S_{c,k,b}]), \qquad b=1,\dots,B.
\end{equation}

The normalized mean absolute errors

\begin{align}
\mathrm{NMAE}^{\mathrm{med}}_c(k) = \frac{1}{s_c B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} |m_{c,k,b}-\tilde m_c|, \qquad
\mathrm{NMAE}^{\mathrm{iqr}}_c(k) = \frac{1}{s_c B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} |q_{c,k,b}-\tilde q_c|
\end{align}

are Monte Carlo estimates~\cite{robert2004monte}, with the reference statistics and scale

\begin{align}
\tilde m_c &= \operatorname{median}(\mathcal{D}_c), &
\tilde q_c &= Q_3(\mathcal{D}_c) - Q_1(\mathcal{D}_c), &
s_c &= \tilde q_c,
\end{align}

where $\mathcal{D}_c = \{x_{c,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ holds the value of the metric of configuration $c$ in each run $i$, taken at the middle batch of the stream. With $N = 1000$, $B = 10^4$ and $k_{\min} = 3$, $k^* = 94$.

\section{Statistical Comparison}
\label{sec:statistical_significance}

Before any test, we discretize the metrics to thresholds of practical relevance, so that floating-point noise and hardware jitter do not inflate the power of the rank-based tests:

\begin{align}
\text{ARI}_\text{disc} &= 10^{-3} \lfloor 10^3 \cdot \text{ARI} \rceil, & \text{Time}_\text{disc} \left[\text{ms}\right] &= 10 \lfloor \text{Time} \left[\text{ms}\right] / 10 \rceil, \nonumber \\
\text{Memory}_\text{disc} \left[\text{B}\right] &= 1024 \lfloor \text{Memory} \left[\text{B}\right] / 1024 \rceil, & \text{MC}_\text{disc} &= \lfloor \text{MC} \rceil.
\end{align}

Every comparison of the thesis sets one model against references, as Dem\v{s}ar recommends when a model is compared with others~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. Two kinds occur:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The model in focus against each other model}, in this chapter and whenever a model is compared with the benchmark models. In each dataset and metric, the model in focus is compared with each other model by the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} over the runs that every model finished, and the $p$-values of these comparisons are adjusted by the Holm--Bonferroni procedure~\cite{holm1979simple}.
    \item \textbf{A variant against its control}, in the improvement of \cref{chap:ModelImprovement}. In each metric, the variant is compared with its control by the same test in each dataset, over the runs that both finished, and the $p$-values of the datasets are adjusted together.
\end{itemize}

With 94 runs, the Wilcoxon test rejects the null hypothesis for differences of no practical consequence, so a difference counts only when it is also large enough. A model $m_i$ is distinguishable from its reference $m_{\text{ref}}$ if

\begin{equation}
D(m_i, m_{\text{ref}}) \iff (p_{\text{adj}} < \alpha) \land (\vert d \vert \ge 0.147),
\end{equation}

where $\alpha = 0.05$, $p_{\text{adj}}$ is the adjusted $p$-value, $d$ is Cliff's delta~\cite{cliff1993dominance}, and $\vert d \vert \ge 0.147$ is the boundary of a non-negligible effect~\cite{romano2006appropriate}.

\paragraph{Reading the tables.} The tables give medians over the runs. In a comparison with the benchmark models, the model in focus comes first, followed by a row that counts the models it is significantly better than, indistinguishable from and worse than. The median of another model is in bold where the model in focus is significantly better than it, and in red where the model in focus is significantly worse. In the tables of a variant and its control, the value of the variant is in bold where it is significantly better than its control, and in red where it is significantly worse. Higher is better for the ARI; lower is better for the execution time, the memory and the size of a summary.

\section{MicroTEDAClus Under the Reassessed Methodology}
\label{sec:reassessment_results}

We evaluate \MicroTEDAClus against the six benchmark models of \cref{sec:benchmark_models} on the six datasets and the seven scalability streams of \cref{sec:datasets}, over 94 runs, under the methodology of the previous sections.

\subsection{Clustering Quality}

\input{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/reassessment/quality.tex}

The results do not support the claims of the original work (\cref{tab:mr_quality}). Against its benchmark models, \MicroTEDAClus is not on par: in the Overall ARI, it is worse than \CluStream and \StreamKM in the three static datasets and worse than \DenStream in \BenchmarkOne, and it is better than \DenStream only in \BenchmarkTwo and \ChameleonDSThree, than \StreamKM only in \BenchmarkTwo and than \CluStream only in \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents. Nor does it adapt better to concept drift. At the penultimate batch, it is better than none of the three in any dataset and worse than \StreamKM in all six. In \BenchmarkOne and \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, two of the streams with concept drift, its median Steady-State ARI is zero, and it is worse than five and three of the six benchmark models, respectively. In the Overall ARI, it is better than most benchmark models only in \BenchmarkTwo and \ChameleonDSThree, mainly the TEDA-based models \AutoCloud, \CEDAS and \TEDACloud, which it beats in every static dataset.

\input{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/reassessment/scalability.tex}

In the scalability streams, the Overall ARI of \MicroTEDAClus stays high at every dimension, but its Steady-State ARI falls with 32 dimensions and reaches zero with 64 (\cref{tab:mr_scalability}): its quality collapses before the end of the stream, which we trace to the dimensionality penalty of \cref{subsubsec:dimensionality_penalty}. From eight dimensions on, it is worse than \CluStream, \StreamKM and \CEDAS in both metrics.

\subsection{Cost}

\input{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/reassessment/cost.tex}

\MicroTEDAClus is slower than every benchmark model but \AutoCloud in the learning stage of every dataset, and slower than \StreamKM and \CEDAS in the prediction stage of every dataset (\cref{tab:mr_cost}). Its maximum memory footprint is larger than those of \DenStream, \StreamKM, \CEDAS and \TEDACloud in every dataset, and larger than that of \CluStream in the Chameleon datasets. These comparisons measure implementations as much as algorithms, and the times are measured inside the tasks, with other tasks running on the same machine.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:reassessment_summary}

The published results of \MicroTEDAClus come from an implementation that departs from its formulation, from benchmark models executed through two layers of interface, from datasets cleaned, scaled with information from the whole stream and ordered by label, and from an evaluation that learns each batch before predicting it. Under a methodology without these flaws, \MicroTEDAClus is not on par with the benchmark models of the original work, it does not adapt better to concept drift, it learns slowly, its quality collapses in high dimensions, and its variance limit depends on the scale of the data (\cref{subsubsec:scaling}). \Cref{chap:ModelImprovement} addresses these weaknesses.
