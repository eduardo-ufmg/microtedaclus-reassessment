\chapter{Methodology Reassessment}
\label{chap:methodology_reassessment}

Maia et al.~\cite{maia2020evolvclus} report that \MicroTEDAClus performs on par with established stream clustering algorithms and adapts better than them to concept drift. In this chapter, we examine what produced those results, from the implementation of the model to the evaluation protocol, define the methodology that the rest of the thesis uses, and evaluate \MicroTEDAClus against six benchmark models under it.

\section{Model Implementation}
\label{sec:model_implementation}

The original work points to a public repository with the code of its experiments, \url{https://github.com/cseveriano/evolving_clustering}, which implements \MicroTEDAClus in \codename{src/models/evolving/EvolvingClustering.py}. To preserve it, we forked the repository at its last commit, \codename{50dd2b4}~\cite{eduardo2026evolvingclustering}.

This implementation is not the formulation of the paper, but it is the code that produced the published results. It shares features with the updated formulation of Galindo~\cite{galindo2021lastmile}, and it solves the outlier condition of microclusters with two samples (\cref{subsubsec:incorrect_condition_logic}), but it departs from the formulation in the decay of the microclusters, in their activation, in the eccentricity and in the membership.

\paragraph{Decay.} The constructor turns the hyperparameter \codename{decay} into \codename{fading\_factor} $= 1/\text{decay}$, the rate $\rho$ of the decay of Galindo (\cref{eq:decay_logic}). The factor acts in two places. The pruning (\cref{snippet:maia_implementation_prune_microclusters}) lowers, at every update, the vitality of the microclusters that are not active, while Galindo lowers that of the microclusters that the current sample does not update; activation is decided only when the macroclusters are built. The vitality update (\cref{snippet:maia_implementation_update_life}) rewards a microcluster when a sample falls within one standard deviation of its mean and penalizes it otherwise, a rule that appears in neither work and that neither explains.

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Pruning of the microclusters in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_prune_microclusters}]
def prune_micro_clusters(self):
    for mc in self.micro_clusters:
        if not mc["active"]:
            mc["life"] = mc["life"] - self.fading_factor
            if mc["life"] < 0:
                self.micro_clusters.remove(mc)
                if mc in self.changed_micro_clusters:
                    self.changed_micro_clusters.remove(mc)
                self.graph.remove_node(mc["id"])
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Update of the vitality of a microcluster in the public implementation of \MicroTEDAClus}, label={snippet:maia_implementation_update_life}]
def update_life(self, xk, micro_cluster):
    previous_mean = micro_cluster["mean"]
    previous_var = micro_cluster["variance"]
    if previous_var > 0:
        d = EvolvingClustering.get_euclidean_distance(xk, previous_mean)
        dist = np.sqrt(np.sum(d))
        rt = np.sqrt(previous_var)
        micro_cluster["life"] = micro_cluster["life"] + (((rt - dist) / rt) * self.fading_factor)
    else:
        micro_cluster["life"] = 1
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Other departures.} The implementation departs from the paper in four more places, all in the file of the model in the forked repository~\cite{eduardo2026evolvingclustering}. It builds the macroclusters in two passes: it groups the intersecting microclusters into the components of a graph $\mathbb{G}_0$, activates microclusters within each component by \cref{critique:use_of_unstable_density}, removes the inactive ones to obtain a graph $\mathbb{G}_1$, and returns the components of $\mathbb{G}_1$ as the macroclusters, which guards against the collapse of overlapping microclusters into a single macrocluster that the original work anticipates (\cref{info:microcluster_activation_criterion}). Only microclusters with more than two samples can be active, while the mean density is computed over all the microclusters of the component. Its outlier test applies \cref{eq:outlier_condition} from three samples on and, below three, the test of the variance alone of \cref{eq:corrected_condition_logic_for_outliers}. Its eccentricity differs from \cref{eq:recursive_eccentricity} by a factor $4/d^2$ on the second term. Its membership weighs $1 - \zeta$ by the densities of the active microclusters, an expression that cannot be rewritten as the normalized typicality of \cref{eq:normalized_typicality}.

The implementation of the updated formulation, which Galindo provided to us, is not public. It adds partial support for the cosine similarity, refactors the decay and moves the microclusters into a class of their own.

\section{Benchmark Models}
\label{sec:benchmark_models}

The original work compares \MicroTEDAClus with \DenStream~\cite{cao2006density}, \CluStream~\cite{aggarwal2003framework} and \StreamKM++~\cite{ackermann2012streamkmpp}, benchmarks common in the literature on stream clustering~\cite{hyde2017fully,akinosho2023comparative,wang2023data,rezaei2025,mutambik2024,jahn2026goingoffline}. It executes them from \codename{Python} through \codename{rpy2}, which calls the \codename{R} package \codename{streamMOA}, itself a wrapper of the \codename{Java} framework MOA~\cite{streamMOA2024}. These two layers of interface add to the time and memory that the experiment measures. To quantify them, we timed each model and measured its memory on a stream of the Random RBF generator with 100{,}000 samples, 10 dimensions, five classes and 50 clusters, eight times, through the bridge and directly in \codename{Java}, with the settings of \codename{streamMOA}: \CluStream with 100 microclusters and five macroclusters, \DenStream with $\epsilon = 0.02$, $\mu = 1$, $\beta = 0.2$, $\lambda = 0.001$, 100 initial points and an offline factor of 2, and \StreamKM with a coreset of 10{,}000 samples and five clusters. Through the bridge, every model takes much longer, while its memory barely changes (\cref{fig:benchmark_java_bridge}). The comparison of the original work therefore measures the interfaces together with the models, and a fair comparison requires every model to run in the environment of the evaluated one.

\subsection{Static Datasets}
\label{subsubsec:scaling}

The static datasets \datasetname{ST-D1} and \datasetname{ST-D2} of the original work come from the evaluation of the Chameleon algorithm~\cite{karypis1999chameleon}, whose paper names the raw patterns, of 10{,}000 and 8{,}000 samples, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour. We use the raw files \codename{cluto-t7-10k.arff} and \codename{cluto-t8-8k.arff}, and those names. The files of the original repository, \codename{chameleon-ds3-clean.csv} and \codename{chameleon-ds4-clean.csv}, are cleaned of noise, downsampled, scaled and ordered by label (\cref{fig:mr_ds3_preprocessing}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{MethodologyReassessment/figures/raw_ds3.png}
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{MethodologyReassessment/figures/scaled_ds3.png}
    \caption[\ChameleonDSThree raw and after the preprocessing of the original work]{\ChameleonDSThree raw, at left, and after the noise removal, the downsampling to half of its samples and the min-max scaling of the original work, at right. Noise samples are black.}
    \label{fig:mr_ds3_preprocessing}
\end{figure}

Removing the samples labeled as noise is a severe leak~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}: a sample is noise only if it belongs to no cluster, which is known after the clustering, and in a stream the model itself must decide which samples are noise, from the samples it has seen, or not handle noise at all~\cite{akinosho2023comparative,wang2023data,rezaei2025,mutambik2024,jahn2026goingoffline,zhang2022extension,zubaroglu2023emcstream}. Keeping a random half of the samples needs no information about them and is not a leak. Scaling is expected in any application, but the original work computes the minimum and the maximum of each feature over the whole dataset, which leaks the future of the stream. The variance limit that the search selects also follows the scale of the data (\cref{tab:params_microtedaclus}), against the claim of the original work that the model is robust to it, and the sensitivity study of \cref{sec:c_implementation} finds the quality strongly dependent on it. Ordering the samples by label is the most harmful step: it leaks the labels and discards the order of arrival, and, since consecutive samples then share their label, most 100-sample batches hold a single cluster.

The experiments of the original work on \datasetname{ST-D1} and \datasetname{ST-D2} therefore do not evaluate online stream clustering, and we use the raw \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour instead. The third static dataset, \Cassini, comes from the \codename{R} package \codename{mlbench} without preprocessing, but ordered by label; we shuffle it.

\subsection{Concept Drift Datasets}

The streams with concept drift of the original work, \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo and \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, come from \codename{streamMOA}. Its plots suggest that their noise samples, labeled \codename{NA} by the package, were removed. We keep them, labeled $-1$.

\subsection{Scalability Streams}

The scalability experiments of the original work, which profile memory and time as the number of samples and the dimension grow, use synthetic streams of \codename{streamMOA} without noise. We generate them with noise.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:prequential_sequential_offline_evaluation}

The original work states that it applies prequential evaluation, in which ``each instance can be used to test before it is used for training''~\cite[Section 5.2.1]{maia2020evolvclus}, the standard practice for stream clustering~\cite{akinosho2023comparative,wang2023data,rezaei2025,mutambik2024,jahn2026goingoffline}, in line with Dawid's view of inference as forecasting future observations~\cite{dawid1984prequential}. Its code, however, implements no prequential evaluation.

For \datasetname{ST-D1} and \datasetname{ST-D2}, it learns the whole dataset and then predicts the same samples (\cref{snippet:original_work_static_evaluation_protocol}). Even for offline clustering, that is not standard practice~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage,arias2024improved,halkidi2001validation}, which prefers cross-validation~\cite{stone1974cross,kohavi1995study}. The evaluation of the \codename{C} reimplementation of \cref{sec:c_implementation} follows the same protocol for all of its datasets. For the other datasets, a function named \codename{prequential\_evaluation} learns each batch before predicting it (\cref{snippet:original_work_prequential_evaluation_function_steps}), the opposite of prequential evaluation.

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Evaluation of the static datasets in the original work}, label={snippet:original_work_static_evaluation_protocol}]
X = stream_df[['x', 'y']].values
y = stream_df['class'].values
evol_model = EvolvingClustering.EvolvingClustering(variance_limit=0.0006, debug=False)
evol_model.fit(X)
y_hat = evol_model.predict(X)
error = adjusted_rand_score(y, y_hat)
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[float, language=Python, caption={Excerpt of the function named \codename{prequential\_evaluation} in the original work}, label={snippet:original_work_prequential_evaluation_function_steps}]
if elapsed_time:
    time_start = time.time()
method.fit(train_data)
y_hat = method.predict(test_data)
y = labels[test_start:test_end]
\end{lstlisting}

Every experiment of this thesis uses prequential evaluation: each 100-sample batch is predicted before the model learns it, and the predicted labels are compared with the true ones.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}

The original work evaluates clustering quality by the adjusted Rand index (ARI)~\cite{hubert1985ari}, an external index that depends neither on the distribution of the data nor on the number of clusters~\cite{halkidi2001validation}. It summarizes the ARI of a run by its mean and standard deviation over the runs, which hides how the quality evolves along a stream: a curve that improves slowly, one that peaks and degrades, and a random one can share both. We summarize the ARI of a run by two metrics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{Overall ARI}, the median of the ARI of the batches of the evaluation segment;
    \item the \textbf{Steady-State ARI}, the ARI of the penultimate batch, which measures the quality after the stream has run its course and any concept drift has occurred. The last batch is avoided because it may be incomplete.
\end{itemize}

The original work reports the execution time only for its scalability streams, as the mean time per sample against the dimension, and the memory only for one run of \BenchmarkOne. We measure the cost of every model on every dataset:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the \textbf{execution time} of the learning and of the prediction stages, each summed over the batches of the evaluation segment, from the wall clock inside the task. \Cref{subsec:mi_optimization_time} measures the time with each task executed alone;
    \item the \textbf{memory footprint}, the size of the model object after each batch, at its maximum and at the penultimate batch;
    \item the \textbf{microcluster count}, at its maximum and at the penultimate batch, a proxy for the memory that depends less on the implementation. \StreamKM keeps no microclusters and is left out of it.
\end{itemize}

For the scalability streams, we report the quality per dimension and plot the cost against the dimension.

\section{Hyperparameter Selection and Runs}
\label{sec:tuning_and_runs}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}
\label{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}

So that no model is disadvantaged by its hyperparameters, even those whose papers use default values, every run begins with a search on the first 20\% of its stream, by Bayesian optimization~\cite{ozaki2025optunahub}, and the tuned model is evaluated on the remaining 80\%, which the search never sees~\cite{kaufman2012leakage,kapoor2023leakage}. The original work, in contrast, selects the parameters that maximize the score over the whole dataset. The search maximizes the objective of \cref{alg:hyperparameter_tuning_objective_function}: the ARI of a prequential execution on the tuning segment, without its first 5\% of predictions, less a penalty of $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ per second of execution.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Objective of the hyperparameter search}
\label{alg:hyperparameter_tuning_objective_function}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require the tuning segment $(X_{opt}, y_{opt})$, the batch size, the model class and its search space, the current trial
\Ensure the score that the search maximizes
\State Draw the hyperparameters of the trial from the search space
\State Build the model with them
\State Execute it prequentially on the tuning segment, measuring the execution time $t_{total}$
\State Drop the first 5\% of the predictions
\State Compute the ARI $ari$ between the remaining predictions and their labels
\State \Return $ari - \lambda\, t_{total}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The search of each model ends at 100 trials or at 300 seconds, whichever comes first. The penalty and the time limit avoid configurations that buy a small gain in ARI with a large cost, and they keep the experiments feasible: some models are slow enough to make an unrestricted search impossible over the runs that the statistics require, so a slow model may complete fewer trials than a fast one. For the same reason, every dataset has at most 10{,}000 samples.

\subsection{Multi-run Statistics}
\label{subsec:multi_run_statistics}

A run generates its stream from its seed, tunes the model and evaluates it. We aggregate the runs by the median and the interquartile range, and not by the mean and the standard deviation, because the search may end in a poor local optimum and produce outlier runs, to which the median is robust. We choose the number of runs from 1000 runs of \datasetname{SCL-2}, \BenchmarkOne and \BenchmarkTwo, in the ARI, the time, the memory and the microcluster count: for each number of runs $k$, we draw $10^4$ subsets of $k$ runs and measure the mean absolute error of their median against the median of the 1000 runs, normalized by its interquartile range. The number of runs is the knee of the worst of these curves, beyond which more runs bring diminishing returns, as the Kneedle algorithm~\cite{satopaa2011kneedle} finds it: $k^* = 94$.

\section{Statistical Comparison}
\label{sec:statistical_significance}

Before any test, we discretize the metrics to thresholds of practical relevance, the ARI to $10^{-3}$, the times to 10 ms, the memory to 1 KiB and the microcluster count to integers, so that floating-point noise and hardware jitter do not inflate the power of the rank-based tests. Every comparison sets one model against references~\cite{demsar2006statistical}: the model in focus against each other model, wherever a model is compared with the benchmark models, and a variant against its control, in the improvement of \cref{chap:ModelImprovement}. In each dataset and metric, the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual} compares them over the runs that both finished, and the Holm--Bonferroni procedure~\cite{holm1979simple} adjusts the $p$-values of the comparisons of a model in focus, or of the datasets of a variant, together. With 94 runs, the test rejects the null hypothesis for differences of no practical consequence, so a model is distinguishable from its reference only if the adjusted $p$-value is below $\alpha = 0.05$ and Cliff's delta~\cite{cliff1993dominance} reaches $\vert d \vert \ge 0.147$, the boundary of a non-negligible effect~\cite{romano2006appropriate}.

\paragraph{Reading the tables.} The tables give medians over the runs. In a comparison with the benchmark models, the model in focus comes first, followed by a row that counts the models it is significantly better than, indistinguishable from and worse than. The median of another model is in bold where the model in focus is significantly better than it, and in red where the model in focus is significantly worse. In the tables of a variant and its control, the value of the variant is in bold where it is significantly better than its control, and in red where it is significantly worse. Higher is better for the ARI; lower is better for the execution time, the memory and the size of a summary.

\section{MicroTEDAClus Under the Reassessed Methodology}
\label{sec:reassessment_results}

We evaluate \MicroTEDAClus against the six benchmark models of \cref{sec:benchmark_models} on the six datasets and the seven scalability streams of \cref{sec:datasets}, over 94 runs, under the methodology of the previous sections.

\subsection{Clustering Quality}

\input{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/reassessment/quality.tex}

The results do not support the claims of the original work (\cref{tab:mr_quality}). Against its benchmark models, \MicroTEDAClus is not on par: in the Overall ARI, it is worse than \CluStream and \StreamKM in the three static datasets and than \DenStream in \BenchmarkOne, and better than each of the three in at most two datasets. Nor does it adapt better to concept drift. At the penultimate batch, it is better than none of the three in any dataset and worse than \StreamKM in all six. In \BenchmarkOne and \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, two of the streams with concept drift, its median Steady-State ARI is zero, and it is worse than five and three of the six benchmark models, respectively. In the Overall ARI, it is better than most benchmark models only in \BenchmarkTwo and \ChameleonDSThree, mainly the TEDA-based models \AutoCloud, \CEDAS and \TEDACloud, which it beats in every static dataset.

\input{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/reassessment/scalability.tex}

In the scalability streams, the Overall ARI of \MicroTEDAClus stays high at every dimension, but its Steady-State ARI falls with 32 dimensions and reaches zero with 64 (\cref{tab:mr_scalability}): its quality collapses before the end of the stream, which we trace to the dimensionality penalty of \cref{subsubsec:dimensionality_penalty}. From eight dimensions on, it is worse than \CluStream, \StreamKM and \CEDAS in both metrics.

\subsection{Cost}

\input{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/reassessment/cost.tex}

\MicroTEDAClus is slower than every benchmark model but \AutoCloud in the learning stage of every dataset, and slower than \StreamKM and \CEDAS in the prediction stage of every dataset (\cref{tab:mr_cost}). Its maximum memory footprint is larger than those of \DenStream, \StreamKM, \CEDAS and \TEDACloud in every dataset, and larger than that of \CluStream in the Chameleon datasets. These comparisons measure implementations as much as algorithms, and the times are taken inside the tasks, with other tasks running on the same machine.

\subsection{Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:parameter_variability}

\input{MethodologyReassessment/artifacts/parameters/MicroTEDAClus_parameters.tex}

The variance limit selected for \MicroTEDAClus spans four orders of magnitude across the datasets, following the scale of each of them (\cref{tab:params_microtedaclus}), which contradicts the claim of the original work that the model is robust to this hyperparameter. The dispersion of the selected decay is higher in the three streams with concept drift than in the static datasets.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:reassessment_summary}

The published results of \MicroTEDAClus come from an implementation that departs from its formulation, benchmark models executed through two layers of interface, datasets cleaned, scaled with the whole stream and ordered by label, and an evaluation that learns each batch before predicting it. Without these flaws, \MicroTEDAClus is not on par with the benchmark models of the original work, it does not adapt better to concept drift, it learns slowly, its quality collapses in high dimensions, and its variance limit depends on the scale of the data (\cref{tab:params_microtedaclus}). \Cref{chap:ModelImprovement} addresses these weaknesses.
