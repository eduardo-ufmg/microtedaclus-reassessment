\chapter{Model Improvement}
\label{chap:ModelImprovement}

The previous chapters identify issues in the formulation of \MicroTEDAClus, from \cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics,subsubsec:incorrect_condition_logic,subsubsec:dimensionality_penalty}, and undocumented behaviours of its reference implementation, in \cref{sec:model_implementation}. This chapter acts on them in three stages. Although our initial plan was to address the algorithmic formulation first, the excessive runtime that results from the inefficient implementation makes the iterative experimentation unaffordable. Thus, we first optimize the implementation for speed. The second stage evaluates corrections of the identified flaws. Then, we evaluate modifications that go beyond the original formulation and define the model that results from them, \MicroTEDAClusPlus. The chapter closes with the evaluation of that model against \MicroTEDAClus in the main experiment, \cref{sec:mi_main_experiment}. \Cref{sec:mi_design} describes how the experiments are run and how their outcomes are judged.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:mi_design}

\subsection{Variants and Controls}

An experiment compares a \emph{variant} of the model with its \emph{control}. The variant differs from the control by a stated combination of changes, so any difference between their results is attributable to those changes. To make this exact, every variant is a configuration of a single implementation, in which each design choice under study is a switch. With every switch in its default position, that implementation executes the original algorithm.

\subsection{Protocol and Datasets}

Every variant is tuned and evaluated by the protocol of the main experiment. The metrics are those of \cref{sec:evaluation_metrics}, computed by the code that computes them for the main experiment. The experiments use eight streams: the six datasets that are not scalability-oriented, and the two scalability streams of highest dimensionality, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64}. The latter are included because the former are all two-dimensional, and several properties of the model only manifest in higher dimensions. The datasets are those of \cref{sec:datasets}, and the tables of this chapter refer to them by the abbreviations of \cref{tab:mi_dataset_abbreviations}.

\subsection{Development and Validation Runs}
\label{subsec:mi_runs}

A run is identified by an index, which determines the seed of the stream generator and of the hyperparameter search. The main experiment uses the runs 0 to 93. The experiments of this chapter use the runs that follow them, so the streams of the main experiment take no part in the development of the model:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Development runs} (94 to 125, 32 runs): every experiment that compares variants is executed on these runs, and every design choice of this chapter was made from their results.
    \item \textbf{Validation runs} (126 to 219, 94 runs): the models accepted during development, and only those, are evaluated again on these runs.
\end{itemize}

The separation exists because a model that is chosen for scoring best on a set of runs is expected to score somewhat worse on other runs. The streams of the validation runs come from seeds that no development run uses, and the validation runs are executed once, after the accepted models are fixed, so their streams take no part in the choice of the models they measure. Their number is the one that \cref{subsec:multi_run_statistics} establishes for the main experiment. The two Chameleon datasets are an exception: they are fixed files, so every run processes the same stream and their runs differ only in the seed of the hyperparameter search. For them, the validation repeats the search and not the stream.

\subsection{Comparisons and Decision Rule}
\label{subsec:mi_decision_rule}

A variant and its control are compared per dataset and per metric, over the runs they share, with the ingredients of \cref{sec:statistical_significance}. The Friedman test is not applied, since each comparison involves two models. The outcome of a comparison is decided on two metrics, the Overall ARI and the Steady-State ARI. A variant

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{improves} its control ($+$) if it is significantly better in at least one dataset and significantly worse in none;
    \item \textbf{regresses} ($-$) if it is significantly worse in at least one dataset and significantly better in none;
    \item is \textbf{mixed} ($\pm$) if both happen, and \textbf{indistinguishable} ($=$) if neither does.
\end{itemize}

Only a variant that improves its control can replace it. The ARI at the fifth batch and the maximum ARI drop are reported, but do not take part in the decision. The microcluster count and the memory footprint are also reported, in \cref{app:mi_tables}, and never decide an outcome. A value in bold is significantly better than the value of its control, and a value in red is significantly worse.

\section{Implementation Optimization}
\label{sec:mi_optimization}

The reference implementation stores each microcluster as a dictionary in a Python list. For every sample, it iterates over that list and updates one microcluster at a time; it maintains the intersection graph edge by edge in a graph library, and it computes the membership of each sample to each macrocluster in nested loops. Its execution time is dominated by the interpreter, not by the arithmetic of the algorithm.

The optimized implementation, hereafter the \modelname{vectorized} model, stores the population of microclusters as a structure of arrays, with one array per attribute. The algorithm has a part that depends on the order of arrival of the samples: the recursive update of the microclusters, the outlier test, the vitality update and the pruning. This part cannot be vectorized across samples, so it runs in a single compiled kernel that iterates over the samples of a batch and, for each of them, over the arrays. Everything else is expressed as array operations. At each macrocluster update, the intersection graph is rebuilt from the matrix of pairwise distances between the microcluster means, its connected components are extracted by a sparse graph routine, and the membership of a whole batch is obtained from one distance matrix.

The \modelname{vectorized} model executes the algorithm of the reference implementation, but does not reproduce its output exactly. Besides the order of the floating-point operations, one behaviour differs. The pruning routine of the reference implementation, shown in \cref{snippet:maia_implementation_prune_microclusters}, removes elements from the list it is iterating over, so the microcluster that follows a removed one is not visited in that pass. The \modelname{vectorized} model visits every microcluster. The same implementation serves as the engine of the experiments of this chapter: each design choice studied in the remainder of this chapter is an option of it, and its default options are the \modelname{vectorized} model.

\subsection{Clustering Quality}
\label{subsec:mi_optimization_quality}

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/optimization/quality.tex}

\Cref{tab:mi_optimization_quality} compares the \modelname{vectorized} model with the reference implementation, the \modelname{original} model of the tables of this chapter, each tuned and evaluated by the protocol on the development runs. By the decision rule, the \modelname{vectorized} model improves the \modelname{original} one: its Overall ARI is significantly higher in \ChameleonDSThree, and it is not significantly lower in any dataset on either metric. The remaining medians differ slightly, and neither the ARI at the fifth batch nor the maximum ARI drop differs significantly in any dataset. The memory footprint, in \cref{tab:mi_optimization_cost}, is significantly smaller in the four datasets in which the models keep the most microclusters.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/optimization/trials.tex}

The two models do not receive the same search. The search ends at 100 trials or at 300 seconds, whichever comes first, and \cref{tab:mi_optimization_trials} shows that the reference implementation exhausts the time before the trials in \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, the datasets in which it is slowest. In \ChameleonDSThree, where it gains, the \modelname{vectorized} model is tuned with several times the trials of the reference implementation. The gain is consistent with this difference of budget, which the experiment does not separate from the difference of pruning behaviour described above. The speed of an implementation is thus also a search budget.

The optimization therefore preserves the clustering quality of the reference implementation, and the \modelname{vectorized} model replaces it as the reference of the remaining experiments.

\subsection{Execution Time}
\label{subsec:mi_optimization_time}

The durations of \cref{tab:mi_optimization_trials} do not measure the optimization alone. They are taken while other tasks run on the same machine, and the two models are tuned independently, so a difference between them mixes the implementation, the load of the machine and the hyperparameters that each search selected. The speedup is therefore measured separately.

Each implementation replays the evaluation segment of every development run with the same hyperparameters: those tuned for the \modelname{original} model in that run and, in a second measurement, those tuned for the \modelname{vectorized} model. Each task is executed alone, on processor cores of a single kind and with the dynamic frequency scaling of the processor disabled, and the compiled kernel is executed once before being timed. The whole set of tasks is executed three times, and the time of a task is the minimum of its three executions.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/optimization/speedup.tex}

The speedup is verified in both measurements, so it does not depend on which model selected the configuration. Furthermore, \modelname{vectorized} is at least twice as fast as \modelname{original} in every run.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ModelImprovement/artifacts/optimization/plots/speedup.png}
    \caption{Speedup of the \modelname{vectorized} implementation in each development run against the number of microclusters that the configuration of the run keeps.}
    \label{fig:mi_optimization_speedup}
\end{figure}

The speedup grows with the number of microclusters, as \cref{fig:mi_optimization_speedup} shows, and levels off when they pass about a hundred. It is therefore largest in \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, whose tuned configurations keep the most microclusters, and smallest in \BenchmarkOne, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64}, whose configurations keep the fewest. This is the behaviour expected from the two implementations as described above: the cost of the reference implementation is that of interpreted code executed for every microcluster and every sample, while the \modelname{vectorized} implementation has a cost per batch that does not depend on the number of microclusters and dominates when they are few.

The reduction is what makes the remaining stages affordable: the experiments of this chapter execute more than sixteen thousand tuning and evaluation tasks, each with up to 100 trials.

\section{Corrections}
\label{sec:mi_corrections}

The candidate corrections come from the issues raised in the previous chapters and from two behaviours of the reference implementation noticed while it was reimplemented. The incorrect condition for microclusters with two samples, \cref{subsubsec:incorrect_condition_logic}, is not among them, because the reference implementation already applies \cref{eq:corrected_condition_logic_for_outliers}.

\subsection{Candidate Corrections}
\label{subsec:mi_correction_candidates}

\paragraph{Membership.} The reference implementation assigns a sample $x$ to the macrocluster that maximizes $\sum_{l} w^l \left(1 - \zeta^l(x)\right)$ over its active microclusters, see \cref{snippet:maia_implementation_calculate_membership}, where $w^l$ is the share of the microcluster in the density of the macrocluster. For a microcluster with $S$ samples, mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,

\begin{equation}
    1 - \zeta(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{2S} \left( 1 + \frac{\lVert x - \mu \rVert^2}{\sigma^2} \right)
    \label{eq:mi_implemented_typicality}
\end{equation}

The standardized squared distance is divided by the number of samples, so the more samples a microcluster has, the less the distance to it matters. A sample five standard deviations away from a microcluster with 500 samples scores $0.974$ in \cref{eq:mi_implemented_typicality}, more than the $0.950$ scored by a sample one standard deviation away from a microcluster with 20 samples. Replacing $1 - \zeta$ by the normalized typicality of the formulation, $t = (1 - \xi)/(S - 2)$, reverses the bias instead of removing it: the values scale with $1/S$, and the microclusters with few samples dominate. The quantity that does not depend on the number of samples is the standardized squared distance itself,

\begin{equation}
    q(x) = S\,\xi(x) - 1 = \frac{\lVert x - \mu \rVert^2}{\sigma^2}
    \label{eq:mi_standardized_distance}
\end{equation}

which is also the quantity that the outlier condition thresholds, since \cref{eq:outlier_condition} is equivalent to $q(x) > m^2$. Five alternatives to the implemented membership are evaluated:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \modelname{norm-typicality}: the implemented weights with the normalized typicality $t^l(x)$, as in \cref{eq:macrocluster_typicality}.
    \item \modelname{local-weights}: \cref{eq:macrocluster_typicality} with the density of each microcluster evaluated at the sample being classified, $D^l(x) = 1/\zeta^l(x)$, which is how the formulation defines the density in \cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics}.
    \item \modelname{density-sum}: the sum of the densities at the sample, $\sum_l D^l(x)$, with $D^l(x) = 2S^l / (1 + q^l(x))$.
    \item \modelname{density-max}: the largest density at the sample, $\max_l D^l(x)$.
    \item \modelname{nearest}: the smallest standardized distance, $\min_l q^l(x)$. The sample is assigned to the macrocluster of the active microcluster it is closest to in standard deviations.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Density.} \Cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics} shows that the density kept by each microcluster is the density of the last sample it absorbed. Two densities that are properties of the microcluster are evaluated. The first, \modelname{peak-density}, is the density of the formulation evaluated at the mean of the microcluster: since $\xi(\mu) = 1/S$,

\begin{equation}
    D = \frac{1}{\zeta(\mu)} = 2S
    \label{eq:mi_peak_density}
\end{equation}

It is the largest value the density of the formulation can take, and it depends on the number of samples alone. This is a property of the TEDA variables and not of this particular choice: with the variance $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{j} \lVert x_j - \mu \rVert^2$, the mean eccentricity of the samples of a microcluster is

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{S} \sum_{j=1}^{S} \xi(x_j) = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{j=1}^{S} \left( \frac{1}{S} + \frac{\lVert x_j - \mu \rVert^2}{S \sigma^2} \right) = \frac{2}{S}
    \label{eq:mi_mean_eccentricity}
\end{equation}

whatever its dispersion. The second, \modelname{volume-density}, divides the number of samples by the variance, $D = S/(\sigma^2 + \epsilon)$, so that dispersion counts. In both, a microcluster with fewer than three samples has no density.

\paragraph{Activation mean.} The reference implementation activates a microcluster whose density is not below the mean density of its component of the intersection graph, and computes that mean over all the microclusters of the component, including those with fewer than three samples, which can never be active. \modelname{mature-mean} computes it over the microclusters with at least three samples.

\paragraph{Initial state.} The reference implementation creates a microcluster flagged as active. Since only inactive microclusters lose vitality, a new microcluster is exempt from the decay until the next macrocluster update. \modelname{born-inactive} creates it inactive.

\paragraph{Dimensionality penalty.} \modelname{no-penalty} removes the factor $2/d$ of \cref{critique:dimensionality_penalty}. The factor cancels in the eccentricity, where it multiplies both the distance and the variance. It acts on the variance that is compared with $r_0$, on the standard deviations of the intersection condition, \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition}, and on the radius of the vitality update of the reference implementation.

\subsection{Combined Corrections}

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/corrections_combined/quality.tex}

With \modelname{nearest} as the new control, \cref{tab:mi_corrections_combined_quality} evaluates whether a second correction improves it. None does. Adding \modelname{peak-density} repeats the loss in \ChameleonDSThree. Adding \modelname{no-penalty}, which improved the \modelname{vectorized} model on its own, lowers the Overall ARI of \datasetname{SCL-32}. The combinations of three and four corrections inherit these losses.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/corrections_combined/partitions.tex}

\Cref{tab:mi_corrections_combined_partitions} shows what \modelname{no-penalty} loses in \datasetname{SCL-32}, through the partitions that the two models predict. It reports their homogeneity, which is one when every predicted cluster contains samples of a single true class, and their completeness, which is one when every true class is contained in a single predicted cluster \cite{rosenberg2007vmeasure}. The stream has three clusters and noise, which counts as a fourth class. With the penalty, the model predicts more clusters than that, with a high homogeneity: part of the noise forms macroclusters of its own. Without the penalty it predicts about three clusters and its homogeneity falls, because the noise is absorbed by the clusters. The ARI rewards the first behaviour, but the benefit is accidental, since the penalty is not a treatment of noise.

The corrected model is therefore the \modelname{vectorized} model with the \modelname{nearest} membership, and it is the control of the experiments that follow. The order-dependent density and the dimensionality penalty remain in it: the experiments do not support replacing the former, and removing the latter costs the accidental separation of the noise.

\section{Improvements}
\label{sec:mi_improvements}

The candidates of this section modify the algorithm beyond what its formulation states. Most of them answer to what the corrected model still does wrong, which \cref{tab:mi_diagnosis_c_nearest} summarizes through the partitions it predicts.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/diagnosis/c_nearest.tex}

Three patterns stand out. In the static shapes of \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, the model predicts more than twice the true number of clusters, with high homogeneity and low completeness: the clusters it finds are pure, but each true cluster is split into several. In \BenchmarkOne, it predicts fewer clusters than there are, with low homogeneity: the two moving clusters are merged. Finally, the model never recognizes noise. Six of the eight datasets contain noise, and every noise sample is assigned to a cluster: the noise that the table reports as predicted comes from the first batch alone, which is predicted before any sample is learned.

A second limitation concerns the hyperparameter search. \Cref{tab:mi_search_range_quality} compares the corrected model with \modelname{wide-range}, the same model with the upper bound of the search range of the variance limit extended from $10^2$ to $10^4$.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/search_range/quality.tex}
\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/search_range/selection.tex}

By the decision rule the wider range improves the corrected model, in \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour. \Cref{tab:mi_search_range_selection} shows that the limits selected for these two datasets are far above the original bound, and several orders of magnitude above those selected for the other two-dimensional datasets: the variance limit depends on the scale of the data. The wider range is nevertheless not adopted. It changes the search and not the model, no fixed range suits every scale, and it has a cost that the same tables show: with limits of that size, more than half of the runs of \ChameleonDSFour end with a single predicted cluster, and the maximum ARI drop of that dataset becomes significantly larger. One of the candidates of this section removes the dependence on the scale in the model instead.

\subsection{Candidate Improvements}
\label{subsec:mi_improvement_candidates}

\paragraph{Formation of the microclusters.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \modelname{variance-cap}: the variance limit $r_0$ only constrains a microcluster with two samples; from the third sample on, a microcluster grows for as long as the samples pass the eccentricity test. This variant also treats as an outlier any sample that would raise the variance of a microcluster above $r_0$, whatever its size, which bounds every microcluster.
    \item \modelname{relative-limit}: $r_0$ becomes a fraction of the variance of the stream, estimated recursively from the samples seen so far, and is searched in $[10^{-6}, 1]$. The hyperparameter no longer depends on the scale of the data, the limitation that \cref{tab:mi_search_range_selection} exposes.
    \item \modelname{single-assign}: a sample updates every microcluster that accepts it, so overlapping microclusters absorb the same samples. This variant updates only the accepting microcluster whose mean is nearest.
    \item \modelname{forgetting}: the sample count used by the recursive mean and variance is capped at $S_{\max}$, searched in $[10, 10^4]$, which makes them exponentially weighted estimates able to follow a moving cluster.
    \item \modelname{chi-threshold}: for a Gaussian cluster in $d$ dimensions, the standardized distance $q$ of \cref{eq:mi_standardized_distance} has mean $1$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{2/d}$. This variant replaces the outlier condition $q > m^2$ by $q > 1 + m\sqrt{2/d}$ and removes the dimensionality penalty, as a principled treatment of the dimension.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pruning.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \modelname{recency}: the rule of \cite{galindo2021lastmile}, \cref{eq:decay_logic}: every microcluster that the current sample does not update loses vitality, and a microcluster that absorbs a sample recovers it in full.
    \item \modelname{recency-inactive}: the same rule restricted to the inactive microclusters.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Formation of the macroclusters.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \modelname{soft-activation}: a microcluster is active if its density is at least $f$ times the mean density of its component, with $f$ searched in $[0, 1.5]$. The original criterion is $f = 1$.
    \item \modelname{no-activation}: every microcluster with at least three samples is active.
    \item \modelname{single-graph}: the macroclusters are the components of the intersection graph $\mathbb{G}_0$ that contain active microclusters, instead of the components of the active graph $\mathbb{G}_1$ of \cref{alg:microcluster_activation_criterion_pseudocode}.
    \item \modelname{link-factor}: the factor $2$ of the intersection condition, \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition}, becomes a hyperparameter $\ell$, searched in $[0.5, 4]$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Prediction.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \modelname{noise-label}: a sample whose standardized distance to every active microcluster exceeds $n_\sigma^2$ is labelled as noise, with $n_\sigma$ searched in $[1, 10]$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sensitivity function.} Three reparameterizations of the function $m$ of \cref{eq:m_function}, each replacing its fixed constants by hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \modelname{m-reparam}: the function
    \begin{equation}
        m(S) = m_{\max} \left( 1 + (m_{\max} - 1) \left( 19 (m_{\max} - 1) \right)^{-(S - 3)/(S_{95} - 3)} \right)^{-1}
        \label{eq:mi_m_reparam}
    \end{equation}
    which starts at $m(3) = 1$ and reaches $0.95\,m_{\max}$ at $S = S_{95}$, with $m_{\max} \in [1.05, 10]$ and $S_{95} \in [5, 5000]$.
    \item \modelname{m-constant}: a constant $m$, searched in $[1, 5]$.
    \item \modelname{m-asymptote}: the original sigmoid with its asymptote, $3$, searched in $[1.05, 10]$.
\end{itemize}

\Cref{tab:mi_improvements_single_quality} compares each candidate with the corrected model. Two candidates improve it. \modelname{single-assign} gains in the Overall ARI of \BenchmarkTwo, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64} and in the Steady-State ARI of \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64}, and \modelname{variance-cap} gains in the Steady-State ARI of \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents. \Cref{tab:mi_improvements_single_quality_other} gives the two ARI metrics that do not decide.

Two other candidates produce the largest gains of the table together with a loss, so the rule classifies them as mixed. \modelname{noise-label} raises the Overall ARI of \BenchmarkOne and that of \datasetname{SCL-64}, and loses in \ChameleonDSThree. \modelname{relative-limit} gains in \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo and \ChameleonDSThree, and loses in the Steady-State ARI of \ChameleonDSFour.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/improvements_single/noise_label.tex}
\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/improvements_single/degenerate.tex}

Both losses have identifiable causes. \Cref{tab:mi_improvements_single_noise_label} shows that, in the two Chameleon datasets, the search selects for \modelname{noise-label} a variance limit and a noise distance at the upper end of their ranges, and that the model still labels as noise more than twice the share of true noise. Its microclusters are too small for the scale of these datasets, the limitation of the search range seen above, which \modelname{relative-limit} removes. \Cref{tab:mi_improvements_single_degenerate} shows that \modelname{relative-limit} ends about a third of the runs of \ChameleonDSFour with a single predicted cluster, as \modelname{wide-range} does: without a bound, a variance limit that suits the first samples lets the microclusters grow until they merge the dataset, the growth that \modelname{variance-cap} prevents. The four changes are therefore evaluated in combination in \cref{subsec:mi_improvements_combined}.

The remaining candidates do not help. \modelname{forgetting} and \modelname{single-graph} are indistinguishable from the control. \modelname{recency} gains in \ChameleonDSFour and loses in \BenchmarkTwo and \Cassini, and \modelname{link-factor} gains in \ChameleonDSFour and loses in \Cassini. \modelname{soft-activation}, \modelname{no-activation} and \modelname{recency-inactive} regress. Every relaxation of the activation criterion is thus neutral or harmful: the two-pass construction of the macroclusters, which \cref{sec:model_implementation} describes as a deviation of the reference implementation, is not a defect to be removed. \modelname{chi-threshold} collapses in every two-dimensional dataset, where \cref{tab:mi_improvements_single_degenerate} shows that it predicts a single cluster in practically every batch. For $d = 2$ its limit is $q \le 1 + m$, which for the values of $m$ close to one that \cref{eq:m_function} gives to young microclusters is twice as permissive as $q \le m^2$, and the microclusters grow until they form a single macrocluster.

\subsection{Covariance of the Microclusters}
\label{subsec:mi_covariance}

Every model evaluated so far describes a microcluster by a scalar variance, which assumes the same dispersion in all directions. This experiment replaces the variance by a covariance matrix and the Euclidean distance by the Mahalanobis distance, which \cref{info:mahalanobis_as_teda_metric} lists among the proximity functions suited to the TEDA metrics.

The covariance follows the recursion of the variance,

\begin{equation}
    \Sigma_S = \frac{S - 1}{S} \Sigma_{S-1} + \frac{1}{S - 1} (x - \mu_S)(x - \mu_S)^T
    \label{eq:mi_covariance_recursion}
\end{equation}

whose trace is the scalar variance, and the standardized distance of \cref{eq:mi_standardized_distance} becomes the Mahalanobis distance divided by the dimension,

\begin{equation}
    q(x) = \frac{1}{d} (x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)
    \label{eq:mi_mahalanobis_distance}
\end{equation}

which coincides with it when the covariance is isotropic. Two properties of the covariance require care.

First, the covariance of a microcluster with few samples is singular. The matrix that is inverted is therefore shrunk towards the isotropic matrix with the same trace, $\tilde{\Sigma} = (1 - \alpha)\,\Sigma + \alpha\,\frac{\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{d}\,I$, with $\alpha = d/(d + S - 1)$. A young microcluster behaves as an isotropic one and becomes anisotropic as it accumulates samples.

Second, the TEDA statistics include the sample under test, and with a full covariance this hides the sample from the test. For a covariance computed with the sample, $(x - \mu)^T \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu) \le S - 1$, so $q(x) \le (S - 1)/d$, and the outlier condition $q(x) > m^2$ cannot hold before the microcluster has $S > 1 + m^2 d$ samples: more than 65 samples in \datasetname{SCL-64}, even with $m = 1$. With the scalar variance the same bound is $q(x) \le S - 1$, which does not involve the dimension. The distance is therefore measured against the covariance before the sample is absorbed, $q_0$, and converted by the identity that relates the two distances in the scalar recursion, $q = c\,q_0 / (1 + c\,q_0/(S - 1))$ with $c = (S - 1)/S$, which keeps the test identical to the isotropic one whenever the covariance is isotropic.

The intersection condition is generalized in two ways. In \modelname{covariance, directional link}, the standard deviation of each microcluster in \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition} is taken along the line that joins the two means, $s_i^2 = e^T \tilde{\Sigma}_i\, e$ with $e$ the unit vector of that line, and the condition becomes $\lVert \mu_i - \mu_j \rVert \le 2 \sqrt{d}\,(s_i + s_j)$. In \modelname{covariance, Bhattacharyya link}, two microclusters intersect when the Bhattacharyya distance between the Gaussian distributions with their means and covariances is at most $2d$. Both reduce to \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition} for two microclusters with the same isotropic covariance. The Bhattacharyya distance requires a determinant and a linear solve per pair of microclusters, so it is applied for $d \le 16$, and the directional condition is used above that. The prediction uses the nearest microcluster by \cref{eq:mi_mahalanobis_distance}.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/geometry/quality.tex}

Both variants are applied to the final model, and both regress in \cref{tab:mi_geometry_quality}. \modelname{covariance, directional link} loses in \BenchmarkTwo on the two decision metrics, and \modelname{covariance, Bhattacharyya link} loses in its Overall ARI. Neither gains significantly in any dataset. In the static shapes of \Cassini, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, where anisotropic microclusters could be expected to follow elongated clusters, no difference is significant, and in \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64} the final model already has a median of $1.000$, which leaves no room for a gain.

\Cref{tab:mi_geometry_cost} shows that the covariance is also costly. A microcluster stores $d^2$ values instead of one, which multiplies the steady-state memory footprint of \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64} by one to two orders of magnitude.

The experiment does not identify why the covariance fails to help. Two explanations are compatible with its results, and neither was tested. The tuned models describe each cluster with many microclusters, so the shape of a cluster may already be expressed by the arrangement of its microclusters and not need to be expressed by the shape of each one. And a covariance matrix has $d(d+1)/2$ values to be estimated from the samples that, in the original model, estimate a single variance. The scalar variance is retained.

\section{Limits of the Hyperparameter Selection}
\label{sec:mi_selection}

The final model stays below an Overall ARI of $0.5$ in the three datasets of static shapes, \Cassini, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, as \cref{tab:mi_validation_quality} shows. Every result of this chapter measures a model as the protocol tunes it, so a low score has two possible origins: the model is not able to do better, or it is, with hyperparameters that the search does not select. This section separates the two for the final model.

\subsection{Reachability Probe}
\label{subsec:mi_probe}

The probe draws 400 configurations at random from a search space, with the distributions that the search itself samples from, and scores each of them twice on a stream. The first score is the objective of the hyperparameter search, computed on the first 20\% of the stream by \cref{alg:hyperparameter_tuning_objective_function}: it is what the search sees. The second is the set of evaluation metrics, computed on the remaining 80\%: it is what the search is meant to maximize.

Two spaces are probed. The first is the search space of the final model, with its three hyperparameters. The second, the \emph{extended} space, adds to them six design choices studied in this chapter, as categorical hyperparameters: \modelname{single-assign} and \modelname{variance-cap}, each on or off; the density of the last absorbed sample or \modelname{peak-density}; the original pruning rule, \modelname{recency} or \modelname{recency-inactive}; the activation factor $f \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$; and the link factor $\ell \in \{1, 1.5, 2\}$. In \Cassini, each configuration is scored on the streams of the first four development runs, and its scores are averaged. \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour are the fixed files presented in \cref{subsec:mi_runs} and offer a single stream each.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/selection/probe.tex}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ModelImprovement/artifacts/selection/plots/probe.png}
    \caption{Objective of the hyperparameter search against Overall ARI of the 400 configurations drawn from the search space of the final model. The colour gives the number of microclusters at the penultimate batch.}

\Cref{tab:mi_selection_probe,fig:mi_selection_probe} show that the search space of the final model holds configurations far better than those the protocol selects. The best configuration has an Overall ARI above one half in \Cassini, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, where the final model as the protocol tunes it stays below it.

The objective does not rank them high. The best configuration of each dataset has less than half of the objective of the configuration that the objective prefers, and the evaluation scores of the latter are close to those of the protocol. The search therefore does what it is asked to do: it finds the configurations that the objective prefers. Over the whole space the two scores are positively correlated because most configurations fail under both. It is among the configurations that work that the objective and the evaluation disagree.

The two kinds of configuration differ in granularity. Those that the objective prefers have higher relative variance limit and a lower decay. The best ones have a lower variance limit and a decay in the thousands, and keep far more microclusters.

\Cref{tab:mi_selection_maturation} follows the two configurations along the stream and shows why the objective prefers the coarse one. In the first fifth of the stream, which is the segment that the search sees, the coarse configuration is the better of the two in the three datasets. From then on it declines, while the fine-grained configuration improves. The second and third panels of the table show what changes. In the first fifth the fine-grained configuration predicts about three times the true number of clusters or more: its many small microclusters are not yet connected into macroclusters. As the stream advances its microclusters multiply and that number falls towards the true one. In \ChameleonDSThree it falls below the true number, and the advantage of the fine-grained configuration is less regular there, with a higher score than the coarse configuration in the third and last fifths and a lower one in the second and fourth. The objective is thus not wrong about the segment it observes. It observes a segment in which the configuration that is better in the long run has not matured. The penalty that the objective applies to the execution time does not explain the preference: it amounts to $10^{-3}$ per second of execution, and these executions take at most a few seconds.

The extended space holds better configurations still, and the objective profits even less from it. A larger space searched with the same objective does not lead to better models, which agrees with the outcome of the reparameterizations of the sensitivity function in \cref{subsec:mi_m_function}. The probe draws the six design choices at random together with the three hyperparameters, and is not designed to attribute the advantage of the best configurations to any of them. What these configurations share with the best ones of the first space is a small variance limit and hundreds of microclusters.

\subsection{Consequences}
\label{subsec:mi_selection_consequences}

Three consequences follow.

First, the modest scores of the final model in the static shapes are, at least in part, a limitation of the selection of its hyperparameters and not of the algorithm. With a fine-grained configuration the model separates the three shapes of \Cassini almost completely, on four different streams, and roughly doubles its Overall ARI in \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour. The figures of \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour are optimistic, since the best of 400 configurations is chosen on the stream on which it is scored and there is no second stream to confirm it.

Second, every comparison of this chapter is conditional on the protocol. A candidate was accepted if it improved its control when both are tuned by the protocol, so a candidate whose benefit requires a configuration that the objective does not select was rejected. The same holds for the baseline and benchmark models of the main experiment, which are tuned by the same protocol. The protocol was kept unchanged throughout this chapter for that reason: it is what places \MicroTEDAClusPlus and the benchmark models on equal terms.

Third, the search is itself a source of variation. Two searches of one same algorithm with the same seeds produce medians that differ in the Overall ARI and in the Steady-State ARI (\cref{subsec:mi_refinements}). In \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, where every run processes the same stream, the dispersion of the results across runs is entirely that of the search.

The probe does not change the model. It indicates that the most direct way of improving its results in static shapes is not a change of the algorithm but an objective that observes a longer segment of the stream.

\section{The Final Model}
\label{sec:mi_final_model}

\MicroTEDAClusPlus keeps the structure of \MicroTEDAClus: microclusters updated recursively with the TEDA statistics, macroclusters as connected components of the graph of intersecting active microclusters, and pruning of the inactive microclusters by vitality. It differs from the algorithm of the reference implementation in the five points that the experiments accepted, listed in \cref{tab:mi_final_differences}. It has three hyperparameters: the relative variance limit $r$, the decay, with $\rho = 1/\text{decay}$, and the noise distance $n_\sigma$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Differences between \MicroTEDAClusPlus and the algorithm of the reference implementation of \MicroTEDAClus.}
\begin{tabular}{l p{0.36\textwidth} p{0.36\textwidth}}
\toprule

\midrule
Membership & macrocluster with the largest density-weighted sum of $1 - \zeta$ & macrocluster of the active microcluster with the smallest standardized distance \\
Update & every microcluster that accepts the sample & the nearest microcluster that accepts the sample \\

\subsection{Microclusters}

A microcluster $i$ is described by its number of samples $S^i$, its mean $\mu^i$, its variance $(\sigma^i)^2$, its density $D^i$, its vitality $v^i$ and a flag that tells whether it is active. The model also keeps the variance of the stream, $\sigma^2_{\text{stream}}$, the mean squared distance of the samples seen so far to their mean, updated before each batch is learned.

For a sample $x$ and a microcluster $i$, let the statistics that the microcluster would have after absorbing the sample be

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    S' = S^i + 1, \qquad \mu' = \frac{S' - 1}{S'} \mu^i + \frac{x}{S'}, \qquad \sigma'^2 = \frac{S' - 1}{S'} (\sigma^i)^2 + \frac{\lVert x - \mu' \rVert^2}{S' - 1}, \\
    \xi' = \frac{1}{S'} + \frac{\lVert x - \mu' \rVert^2}{S' \sigma'^2}
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:mi_tentative_update}
\end{equation}

with $\xi' = 1/S'$ when $\sigma'^2 = 0$. The sample is an outlier to the microcluster if

\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
        \sigma'^2 > r\,\sigma^2_{\text{stream}}, & S' < 3 \\[4pt]
        \dfrac{\xi'}{2} > \dfrac{m(S')^2 + 1}{2 S'}, & S' \ge 3
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:mi_final_outlier}
\end{equation}

where $m$ is the function of \cref{eq:m_function}. \Cref{alg:mi_final_microclusters} processes a sample.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Microcluster update of \MicroTEDAClusPlus for a sample $x$}
\label{alg:mi_final_microclusters}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x$, the microclusters, $r$, $\rho$, $\sigma^2_{\text{stream}}$
\State $A \gets$ the microclusters to which $x$ is not an outlier by \cref{eq:mi_final_outlier}
\If{$A = \emptyset$}
    \State create a microcluster with $S = 1$, $\mu = x$, $\sigma^2 = 0$, $D = 0$, $v = 1$, flagged as active
\Else
    \State $i \gets \arg\min_{l \in A} \lVert x - \mu^l \rVert$
    \State $v^i \gets v^i + \rho\,(\sigma^i - \lVert x - \mu^i \rVert)/\sigma^i$ if $\sigma^i > 0$, otherwise $v^i \gets 1$
    \State $S^i \gets S'$, $\mu^i \gets \mu'$, $(\sigma^i)^2 \gets \sigma'^2$, $D^i \gets 2/\xi'$, from \cref{eq:mi_tentative_update}
\EndIf
\For{each microcluster $l$ that is not active}
    \State $v^l \gets v^l - \rho$
    \State remove $l$ if $v^l < 0$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Macroclusters}

The macroclusters are rebuilt after each batch. Two microclusters intersect when \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition} holds between their means and standard deviations, which defines the graph $\mathbb{G}_0$. In each connected component of $\mathbb{G}_0$, a microcluster is active if it has at least three samples and its density is not below the mean density of the microclusters of the component. The macroclusters are the connected components of $\mathbb{G}_1$, the subgraph of $\mathbb{G}_0$ induced by the active microclusters.

\subsection{Prediction}

Let $q^l(x) = \lVert x - \mu^l \rVert^2 / (\sigma^l)^2$ be the standardized squared distance of a sample to an active microcluster $l$, and $l^* = \arg\min_l q^l(x)$. The sample is labelled as noise if $q^{l^*}(x) > n_\sigma^2$, and receives the label of the macrocluster of $l^*$ otherwise. Before the first batch is learned there is no active microcluster, and every sample is labelled as noise.

\subsection{What Remains of the Original Model}

The function $m$, the density, the activation criterion, the two-pass construction of the macroclusters and the vitality mechanism are those of the reference implementation. Each of them was challenged in this chapter by at least one alternative and none of the alternatives improved the model. In particular, the density of a microcluster is still the density of the last sample it absorbed, the order dependence identified in \cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics}: in \MicroTEDAClusPlus it only decides which microclusters are active, and \cref{subsec:mi_density_revisited} verifies whether an order-independent density can replace it there.

The hyperparameters are searched in $r \in [10^{-6}, 1]$ and $\text{decay} \in [10, 10^4]$, both on a logarithmic scale, and $n_\sigma \in [1, 10]$.

The experiments of this chapter execute the final model as a configuration of the implementation of \cref{sec:mi_optimization}. For the main experiment, the model is implemented on its own, with its three hyperparameters and without the options of the experimental implementation. The two implementations were verified to produce the same predictions and the same number of microclusters, batch by batch, on scalability streams with 2, 8 and 32 dimensions.

\section[\MicroTEDAClusPlus in the Main Experiment]{\MicroTEDAClusPlus and \MicroTEDAClus in the Main Experiment}
\label{sec:mi_main_experiment}

The experiments above compare models that share one implementation, on runs reserved for the development of the model. This section returns to the main experiment of \cref{chap:methodology_reassessment}. \MicroTEDAClusPlus is tuned and evaluated on its 94 runs and 13 datasets, by the same code and on the same machine as the benchmark models, and is compared with the \MicroTEDAClus of \cref{sec:reassessment_results}, the reference implementation. The comparison with the remaining benchmark models is the subject of \cref{chap:results}.

The streams of these runs took no part in the development of the model, as required by \cref{subsec:mi_runs}, with one exception: \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour are fixed files, so their streams are the same in every run. The results of the two models are compared by the analysis of the main experiment, \cref{sec:statistical_significance}, in which the Friedman test is skipped because only two models are involved. In the tables of this section, a value in bold is significantly better than that of the other model, a value in red is significantly worse, and two values without emphasis are not significantly different.

\subsection{Summary}

Under the protocol of the main experiment, \MicroTEDAClusPlus improves on \MicroTEDAClus in the following terms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clustering quality.} The Overall ARI is significantly higher in five of the six non-scalability datasets and the Steady-State ARI in all six; the maximum ARI drop is significantly smaller in all six. In the scalability streams, the four ARI metrics are significantly better from two dimensions on, and the degradation of \MicroTEDAClus along high-dimensional streams does not occur.
    \item \textbf{Cost.} The fitting and prediction times and the memory footprint are significantly smaller in every dataset.
\end{itemize}

It does not improve on it everywhere. The Overall ARI of \Cassini and the four ARI metrics of the one-dimensional stream are not significantly different, the latter with lower medians. The scores in the three datasets of static shapes remain at or below $0.5$, for the reason examined in \cref{sec:mi_selection}. And in \BenchmarkOne the model keeps two to four times as many microclusters as the baseline.
