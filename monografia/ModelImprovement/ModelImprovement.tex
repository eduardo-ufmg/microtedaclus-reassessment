\chapter{Model Improvement}
\label{chap:ModelImprovement}

\Cref{chap:background_relatedwork,chap:methodology_reassessment} identify issues in the formulation of \MicroTEDAClus (\cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics,subsubsec:incorrect_condition_logic,subsubsec:dimensionality_penalty}) and undocumented behaviors of its public implementation (\cref{sec:model_implementation}). In this chapter, we act on them in three stages. We first optimize the implementation, whose execution time made iterative experimentation unaffordable. We then evaluate corrections of the flaws, and finally modifications beyond the original formulation, which define \MicroTEDAClusPlus. We close the chapter by comparing \MicroTEDAClusPlus with \MicroTEDAClus and the benchmark models in the main experiment (\cref{sec:mi_main_experiment}).

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:mi_design}

\subsection{Variants and Controls}

An experiment compares a \emph{variant} of the model with its \emph{control}. The variant differs from the control by a stated combination of changes, so any difference between their results is attributable to those changes. To make this exact, we implement every variant as a configuration of a single implementation, in which each design choice under study is a switch. With every switch in its default position, that implementation executes the original algorithm.

\subsection{Protocol and Datasets}

We tune and evaluate every variant by the protocol of the main experiment, with the metrics of \cref{sec:evaluation_metrics}. We use eight streams: the six datasets of the main experiment and its two scalability streams of highest dimension, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64}, because the six datasets are two-dimensional and several properties of the model only show in higher dimensions. The tables use the abbreviations of \cref{tab:synthetic_datasets}.

\subsection{Development and Validation Runs}
\label{subsec:mi_runs}

A run is identified by an index, which determines the seed of the stream generator and of the hyperparameter search. The main experiment uses the runs 0 to 93. In this chapter, we use the runs that follow them, so the streams of the main experiment take no part in the development of the model:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Development runs} (94 to 125, 32 runs): we execute every experiment that compares variants on these runs, and we made every design choice of this chapter from their results.
    \item \textbf{Validation runs} (126 to 219, 94 runs): we evaluate again on these runs the models accepted during development, and only those.
\end{itemize}

A model chosen for scoring best on a set of runs is expected to score somewhat worse on others. The validation runs come from seeds that no development run uses, and we executed them once, after fixing the accepted models, so their streams take no part in the choices they measure; their number is that of \cref{subsec:multi_run_statistics}. The Chameleon datasets are an exception: they are fixed files, so their runs differ only in the seed of the search, and the validation repeats the search and not the stream.

\subsection{Comparisons and Decision Rule}
\label{subsec:mi_decision_rule}

We compare a variant with its control in each dataset and metric, over the runs they share, by the test of \cref{sec:statistical_significance}, whose conventions the tables follow, and decide the outcome on two metrics, the Overall and the Steady-State ARI. A variant

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{improves} its control if it is significantly better in at least one dataset and significantly worse in none;
    \item \textbf{regresses} if it is significantly worse in at least one dataset and significantly better in none;
    \item is \textbf{mixed} if both happen, and \textbf{indistinguishable} if neither does.
\end{itemize}

Only a variant that improves its control can replace it. The microcluster count and the memory footprint never decide an outcome.

\section{Implementation Optimization}
\label{sec:mi_optimization}

The reference implementation stores each microcluster as a dictionary in a Python list. For every sample, it iterates over that list and updates one microcluster at a time; it maintains the intersection graph edge by edge in a graph library, and it computes the membership of each sample to each macrocluster in nested loops. Its execution time is dominated by the interpreter, not by the arithmetic of the algorithm. The reimplementation of \cref{sec:c_implementation} reduces it by changing the language. We keep \codename{Python}, the language of the benchmark models, and change how the algorithm is computed.

The optimized implementation, hereafter the \variant{vectorized} model, stores the population of microclusters as a structure of arrays, with one array per attribute. The algorithm has a part that depends on the order of arrival of the samples: the recursive update of the microclusters, the outlier test, the vitality update and the pruning. This part cannot be vectorized across samples, so it runs in a single compiled kernel that iterates over the samples of a batch and, for each of them, over the arrays. Everything else is expressed as array operations. At each macrocluster update, the intersection graph is rebuilt from the matrix of pairwise distances between the microcluster means, its connected components are extracted by a sparse graph routine, and the membership of a whole batch is obtained from one distance matrix.

The \variant{vectorized} model executes the algorithm of the reference implementation, but does not reproduce its output exactly. Besides the order of the floating-point operations, one behavior differs. The pruning routine of the reference implementation, shown in \cref{snippet:maia_implementation_prune_microclusters}, removes elements from the list it is iterating over, so the microcluster that follows a removed one is not visited in that pass. The \variant{vectorized} model visits every microcluster. The same implementation serves as the engine of the experiments of this chapter: each design choice studied in the remainder of this chapter is an option of it, and its default options are the \variant{vectorized} model.

\subsection{Clustering Quality}
\label{subsec:mi_optimization_quality}

\Cref{tab:mi_optimization_quality} compares the \variant{vectorized} model with the reference implementation, the \variant{original} model of the tables of this chapter, each tuned and evaluated by the protocol on the development runs. By the decision rule, the \variant{vectorized} model improves the \variant{original} one: its Overall ARI is significantly higher in \ChameleonDSThree, and it is not significantly lower in any dataset on either metric. \input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/optimization/trials.tex}

The two models do not receive the same search. The search ends at 100 trials or at 300 seconds, whichever comes first, and \cref{tab:mi_optimization_trials} shows that the reference implementation exhausts the time before the trials in \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, the datasets in which it is slowest. In \ChameleonDSThree, where it gains, the \variant{vectorized} model is tuned with several times the trials of the reference implementation. The gain is consistent with this difference of budget, which the experiment does not separate from the difference of pruning behavior described above. The speed of an implementation is thus also a search budget.

The optimization therefore preserves the clustering quality of the reference implementation, and the \variant{vectorized} model replaces it as the reference of the remaining experiments.

\subsection{Execution Time}
\label{subsec:mi_optimization_time}

The durations of \cref{tab:mi_optimization_trials} do not measure the optimization alone. They are taken while other tasks run on the same machine, and the two models are tuned independently, so a difference between them mixes the implementation, the load of the machine and the hyperparameters that each search selected. We therefore measure the speedup separately.

Each implementation replays the evaluation segment of every development run with the same hyperparameters: those tuned for the \variant{original} model in that run and, in a second measurement, those tuned for the \variant{vectorized} model. We execute each task alone, on identical processor cores, and run the compiled kernel once before timing it. We execute the whole set of tasks three times, and the time of a task is the minimum of its three executions.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/optimization/speedup.tex}

\Cref{tab:mi_optimization_speedup} verifies the speedup in both measurements, so it does not depend on which model selected the configuration, and the \variant{vectorized} model is at least twice as fast as the \variant{original} one in every run.

The reduction is what makes the remaining stages affordable: the experiments of this chapter execute more than sixteen thousand tuning and evaluation tasks, each with up to 100 trials.

\section{Corrections}
\label{sec:mi_corrections}

The candidate corrections come from the issues raised in the previous chapters and from two behaviors of the reference implementation that we noticed while reimplementing it. The incorrect condition for microclusters with two samples, \cref{subsubsec:incorrect_condition_logic}, is not among them, because the reference implementation already applies \cref{eq:corrected_condition_logic_for_outliers}.

\subsection{Candidate Corrections}
\label{subsec:mi_correction_candidates}

\paragraph{Membership.} The reference implementation assigns a sample $x$ to the macrocluster that maximizes $\sum_{l} w^l \left(1 - \zeta^l(x)\right)$ over its active microclusters, see \cref{snippet:maia_implementation_calculate_membership}, where $w^l$ is the share of the microcluster in the density of the macrocluster. For a microcluster with $S$ samples, mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,

\begin{equation}
    1 - \zeta(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{2S} \left( 1 + \frac{\lVert x - \mu \rVert^2}{\sigma^2} \right)
    \label{eq:mi_implemented_typicality}
\end{equation}

The standardized squared distance is divided by the number of samples, so the more samples a microcluster has, the less the distance to it matters. A sample five standard deviations away from a microcluster with 500 samples scores $0.974$ in \cref{eq:mi_implemented_typicality}, more than the $0.950$ scored by a sample one standard deviation away from a microcluster with 20 samples. Replacing $1 - \zeta$ by the normalized typicality of the formulation, $t = (1 - \xi)/(S - 2)$, reverses the bias instead of removing it: the values scale with $1/S$, and the microclusters with few samples dominate. The quantity that does not depend on the number of samples is the standardized squared distance itself,

\begin{equation}
    q(x) = S\,\xi(x) - 1 = \frac{\lVert x - \mu \rVert^2}{\sigma^2}
    \label{eq:mi_standardized_distance}
\end{equation}

which the outlier condition also thresholds, since \cref{eq:outlier_condition} is equivalent to $q(x) > m^2$. We evaluate five alternatives to the membership of the implementation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \variant{norm-typicality}: the implemented weights with the normalized typicality $t^l(x)$, as in \cref{eq:macrocluster_typicality}.
    \item \variant{local-weights}: \cref{eq:macrocluster_typicality} with the density of each microcluster evaluated at the sample being classified, $D^l(x) = 1/\zeta^l(x)$, which is how the formulation defines the density in \cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics}.
    \item \variant{density-sum}: the sum of the densities at the sample, $\sum_l D^l(x)$, with $D^l(x) = 2S^l / (1 + q^l(x))$.
    \item \variant{density-max}: the largest density at the sample, $\max_l D^l(x)$.
    \item \variant{nearest}: the smallest standardized distance, $\min_l q^l(x)$. The sample is assigned to the macrocluster of the active microcluster it is closest to in standard deviations.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Density.} \Cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics} shows that the density kept by each microcluster is the density of the last sample it absorbed. We evaluate two densities that are properties of the microcluster. The first, \variant{peak-density}, is the density of the formulation evaluated at the mean of the microcluster: since $\xi(\mu) = 1/S$,

\begin{equation}
    D = \frac{1}{\zeta(\mu)} = 2S
    \label{eq:mi_peak_density}
\end{equation}

It is the largest value the density of the formulation can take, and it depends on the number of samples alone. This is a property of the TEDA variables and not of this particular choice: with the variance $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{j} \lVert x_j - \mu \rVert^2$, the mean eccentricity of the samples of a microcluster is

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{S} \sum_{j=1}^{S} \xi(x_j) = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{j=1}^{S} \left( \frac{1}{S} + \frac{\lVert x_j - \mu \rVert^2}{S \sigma^2} \right) = \frac{2}{S}
    \label{eq:mi_mean_eccentricity}
\end{equation}

whatever its dispersion. The second, \variant{volume-density}, divides the number of samples by the variance, $D = S/(\sigma^2 + \epsilon)$, so that dispersion counts. In both, a microcluster with fewer than three samples has no density.

\paragraph{Activation mean.} The reference implementation activates a microcluster whose density is not below the mean density of its component of the intersection graph, and computes that mean over all the microclusters of the component, including those with fewer than three samples, which can never be active. \variant{mature-mean} computes it over the microclusters with at least three samples.

\paragraph{Initial state.} The reference implementation creates a microcluster flagged as active. Since only inactive microclusters lose vitality, a new microcluster is exempt from the decay until the next macrocluster update. \variant{born-inactive} creates it inactive.

\paragraph{Dimensionality penalty.} \variant{no-penalty} removes the factor $2/d$ of \cref{critique:dimensionality_penalty}. The factor cancels in the eccentricity, where it multiplies both the distance and the variance. It acts on the variance that is compared with $r_0$, on the standard deviations of the intersection condition, \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition}, and on the radius of the vitality update of the reference implementation.

\subsection{Corrections One at a Time}

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/corrections_single/quality.tex}

\Cref{tab:mi_corrections_single_quality} compares each candidate with the \variant{vectorized} model. Two candidates improve it: \variant{nearest}, in the Overall ARI of \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents and \datasetname{SCL-32} and in the Steady-State ARI of \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, \Cassini, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64}, and \variant{no-penalty}, in the Steady-State ARI of \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64}.

Among the memberships, only the count-free distance helps. \variant{density-max} is indistinguishable from the implemented membership and \variant{density-sum} is worse. The two memberships built on the normalized typicality of the formulation, \variant{norm-typicality} and \variant{local-weights}, gain in the Steady-State ARI of \ChameleonDSFour and \datasetname{SCL-64} and lose in the Overall ARI of \ChameleonDSThree, so the formulation is not preferable to its implementation in this respect.

Neither order-independent density improves the model. \variant{peak-density} is worse in \ChameleonDSThree on both metrics, and \variant{volume-density} trades a gain in the Steady-State ARI of \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents for a loss in the Overall ARI of \Cassini. The order dependence is real, but in this algorithm the density only decides which microclusters are active and how they are weighted, and for those purposes the alternatives are not better. \variant{mature-mean} and \variant{born-inactive} also regress, in \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour respectively.

\subsection{Combined Corrections}

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/corrections_combined/quality.tex}

With \variant{nearest} as the new control, \cref{tab:mi_corrections_combined_quality} evaluates whether a second correction improves it. None does. Adding \variant{peak-density} repeats the loss in \ChameleonDSThree. Adding \variant{no-penalty}, which improved the \variant{vectorized} model on its own, lowers the Overall ARI of \datasetname{SCL-32}. The combinations of three and four corrections inherit these losses.

The corrected model is therefore the \variant{vectorized} model with the \variant{nearest} membership, and it is the control of the experiments that follow. The order-dependent density and the dimensionality penalty remain in it: the experiments support replacing neither.

\section{Improvements}
\label{sec:mi_improvements}

The candidates of this section modify the algorithm beyond what its formulation states. Most of them answer to what the corrected model still does wrong, which \cref{tab:mi_diagnosis_c_nearest} summarizes through the partitions it predicts.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/diagnosis/c_nearest.tex}

Three patterns stand out. In the static shapes of \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, the model predicts more than twice the true number of clusters, with high homogeneity and low completeness: the clusters it finds are pure, but each true cluster is split into several. In \BenchmarkOne, it predicts fewer clusters than there are, with low homogeneity: the two moving clusters are merged. Finally, the model never recognizes noise. Six of the eight datasets contain noise, and every noise sample is assigned to a cluster: the noise that the table reports as predicted comes from the first batch alone, which is predicted before any sample is learned.

A second limitation concerns the hyperparameter search. \Cref{tab:mi_search_range_quality} compares the corrected model with \variant{wide-range}, the same model with the upper bound of the search range of the variance limit extended from $10^2$ to $10^4$.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/search_range/quality.tex}
\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/search_range/selection.tex}

By the decision rule the wider range improves the corrected model, in \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour. \Cref{tab:mi_search_range_selection} shows that the limits selected for these two datasets are far above the original bound, and several orders of magnitude above those selected for the other two-dimensional datasets: the variance limit depends on the scale of the data. We nevertheless do not adopt the wider range. It changes the search and not the model, no fixed range suits every scale, and it has a cost that the same tables show: with limits of that size, more than half of the runs of \ChameleonDSFour end with a single predicted cluster. One of the candidates of this section removes the dependence on the scale in the model instead.

\subsection{Candidate Improvements}
\label{subsec:mi_improvement_candidates}

\paragraph{Formation of the microclusters.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \variant{variance-cap}: the variance limit $r_0$ only constrains a microcluster with two samples; from the third sample on, a microcluster grows for as long as the samples pass the eccentricity test. This variant also treats as an outlier any sample that would raise the variance of a microcluster above $r_0$, whatever its size, which bounds every microcluster.
    \item \variant{relative-limit}: $r_0$ becomes a fraction of the variance of the stream, estimated recursively from the samples seen so far, and is searched in $[10^{-6}, 1]$. The hyperparameter no longer depends on the scale of the data, the limitation that \cref{tab:mi_search_range_selection} exposes.
    \item \variant{single-assign}: a sample updates every microcluster that accepts it, so overlapping microclusters absorb the same samples. This variant updates only the accepting microcluster whose mean is nearest.
    \item \variant{forgetting}: the sample count used by the recursive mean and variance is capped at $S_{\max}$, searched in $[10, 10^4]$, which makes them exponentially weighted estimates able to follow a moving cluster.
    \item \variant{chi-threshold}: for a Gaussian cluster in $d$ dimensions, the standardized distance $q$ of \cref{eq:mi_standardized_distance} has mean $1$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{2/d}$. This variant replaces the outlier condition $q > m^2$ by $q > 1 + m\sqrt{2/d}$ and removes the dimensionality penalty, as a principled treatment of the dimension.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Pruning.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \variant{recency}: the rule of Galindo~\cite{galindo2021lastmile}, \cref{eq:decay_logic}: every microcluster that the current sample does not update loses vitality, and a microcluster that absorbs a sample recovers it in full.
    \item \variant{recency-inactive}: the same rule restricted to the inactive microclusters.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Formation of the macroclusters.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \variant{soft-activation}: a microcluster is active if its density is at least $f$ times the mean density of its component, with $f$ searched in $[0, 1.5]$. The original criterion is $f = 1$.
    \item \variant{no-activation}: every microcluster with at least three samples is active.
    \item \variant{single-graph}: the macroclusters are the components of the intersection graph $\mathbb{G}_0$ that contain active microclusters, instead of the components of the active graph $\mathbb{G}_1$ of \cref{alg:microcluster_activation_criterion_pseudocode}.
    \item \variant{link-factor}: the factor $2$ of the intersection condition, \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition}, becomes a hyperparameter $\ell$, searched in $[0.5, 4]$. Cardoso~\cite{cardoso2022microtedaclus} proposes an earlier condition with a factor, \cref{eq:cardoso_intersection_condition}, which scales the sum of the radii $m\sigma$ of the two microclusters instead of the sum of their standard deviations.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Prediction.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \variant{noise-label}: a sample whose standardized distance to every active microcluster exceeds $n_\sigma^2$ is labeled as noise, with $n_\sigma$ searched in $[1, 10]$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Sensitivity function.} Three variants replace fixed constants of the function $m$ of \cref{eq:m_function} by hyperparameters: \variant{m-reparam} parameterizes it by its maximum and by the number of samples at which it reaches 95\% of it, \variant{m-constant} makes $m$ a constant, and \variant{m-asymptote} searches the asymptote of the original sigmoid.

\subsection{Improvements One at a Time}

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/improvements_single/quality.tex}

\Cref{tab:mi_improvements_single_quality} compares each candidate with the corrected model. Two candidates improve it. \variant{single-assign} gains in the Overall ARI of \BenchmarkTwo, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64} and in the Steady-State ARI of \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64}, and \variant{variance-cap} gains in the Steady-State ARI of \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents.

Two other candidates produce the largest gains of the table together with a loss, so the rule classifies them as mixed. \variant{noise-label} raises the Overall ARI of \BenchmarkOne and that of \datasetname{SCL-64}, and loses in \ChameleonDSThree. \variant{relative-limit} gains in \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo and \ChameleonDSThree, and loses in the Steady-State ARI of \ChameleonDSFour.

The loss of each falls where another candidate acts. \variant{noise-label} loses in \ChameleonDSThree, whose scale the fixed range of the variance limit does not suit (\cref{tab:mi_search_range_selection}), which \variant{relative-limit} adapts to; \variant{relative-limit} loses in \ChameleonDSFour, and nothing in it bounds the growth of a microcluster, which \variant{variance-cap} does. The four changes are therefore evaluated in combination in \cref{subsec:mi_improvements_combined}.

The remaining candidates do not help. \variant{forgetting} and \variant{single-graph} are indistinguishable from the control. \variant{recency} gains in \ChameleonDSFour and loses in \BenchmarkTwo and \Cassini, and \variant{link-factor} gains in \ChameleonDSFour and loses in \Cassini. \variant{soft-activation}, \variant{no-activation} and \variant{recency-inactive} regress. Every relaxation of the activation criterion is thus neutral or harmful: the two-pass construction of the macroclusters, which \cref{sec:model_implementation} describes as a deviation of the reference implementation, is not a defect to be removed. \variant{chi-threshold} collapses in every two-dimensional dataset, where its ARI is zero (\cref{tab:mi_improvements_single_quality}). For $d = 2$ its limit is $q \le 1 + m$, which for the values of $m$ close to one that \cref{eq:m_function} gives to young microclusters is twice as permissive as $q \le m^2$, and the microclusters grow until they form a single macrocluster.

\subsection{Combined Improvements}
\label{subsec:mi_improvements_combined}

We evaluate the four changes that helped in isolation in every combination of two, three and four of them. In the names of the combinations, \variant{single}, \variant{cap}, \variant{noise} and \variant{relative} stand for \variant{single-assign}, \variant{variance-cap}, \variant{noise-label} and \variant{relative-limit}.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/improvements_combined/quality.tex}

\Cref{tab:mi_improvements_combined_quality} shows that nine of the eleven combinations improve the corrected model, and the two that do not, \variant{single + cap} and \variant{cap + noise}, each lose in one dataset, \Cassini and \ChameleonDSThree respectively. The two losses of the previous subsection disappear as expected: \variant{noise + relative} gains in \ChameleonDSThree, where \variant{noise-label} alone lost, and \variant{cap + relative} gains in \ChameleonDSFour, where \variant{relative-limit} alone lost.

Since several combinations improve the control, the decision rule does not single one out. We choose the combination that becomes the improved model in two steps: it must not be improved upon by any other combination in a direct comparison, and, among those that satisfy this, it must have the largest number of significant gains over the corrected model. \variant{single + relative} and \variant{single + noise + relative} have the most gains, twelve each over the two metrics and eight datasets. \Cref{tab:mi_improvements_selection_quality} gives the direct comparisons that decide between the leading combinations.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/improvements_selection/quality.tex}

\variant{single + noise + relative} improves \variant{single + relative}, in \BenchmarkOne, and no combination improves it; its comparison with \variant{noise + relative} is mixed, and adding \variant{variance-cap} to it regresses in \ChameleonDSThree. The only other combination that no other improves, \variant{cap + noise + relative}, has seven gains over the corrected model, and the two differ by a trade-off that the rule does not resolve: the first is better in \BenchmarkOne, the second in \ChameleonDSFour.

The improved model is therefore the corrected model with \variant{single-assign}, \variant{noise-label} and \variant{relative-limit}. Compared with the corrected model, it gains in the Overall ARI of \BenchmarkOne, \BenchmarkTwo, \ChameleonDSThree, \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64} and in the Steady-State ARI of every dataset except \Cassini, where its medians are lower without the difference being significant.

\subsection{Refinements of the Improved Model}
\label{subsec:mi_refinements}

We evaluate four further changes on the improved model. \variant{forgetting} and \variant{recency} did not help the corrected model, but they target the tracking of moving clusters, which the improved model now does better. The removal of the dimensionality penalty improved the \variant{vectorized} model but lowered the Overall ARI of \datasetname{SCL-32} combined with \variant{nearest} (\cref{sec:mi_corrections}); we reconsider it now that the model labels the noise explicitly. \variant{persistent-labels} makes each macrocluster keep the label of the macrocluster with which it shares most of its mass, instead of being renumbered at every update: the partition of a batch does not change, but the objective of the search computes the ARI over all batches together (\cref{subsec:hyperparameter_tuning}), where a renumbering counts as a disagreement.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/refinements/quality.tex}

Only the removal of the penalty improves the improved model in \cref{tab:mi_refinements_quality}: it takes the Overall and Steady-State ARI of \datasetname{SCL-32} and \datasetname{SCL-64} to $1.000$. \variant{persistent-labels} and \variant{forgetting} are indistinguishable from the control, and \variant{recency} regresses in \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour.

The first two rows of each panel also give a measure of the noise of the protocol. In the six two-dimensional datasets the factor $2/d$ is one, so \variant{improved} and \variant{improved + no-penalty} are the same algorithm, tuned twice with the same seeds. Their medians still differ, most of all in \BenchmarkOne and \BenchmarkTwo, and none of these differences is significant.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/refinements/tuning_noise.tex}

\Cref{tab:mi_refinements_tuning_noise} locates the origin of these differences in the search. The two searches select the same hyperparameters in most of the runs of four datasets and in a minority of the runs of \BenchmarkOne and \BenchmarkTwo, and wherever they do, the two evaluations coincide. The sampler of the search is seeded and the algorithm is deterministic, so the execution time that the objective measures is the only input that differs between the two searches. Differences of this size between distinct models should therefore not be read as effects.

The model that results, \MicroTEDAClusPlus, is therefore the improved model without the dimensionality penalty.

\subsection{Variants Not Retained}
\label{subsec:mi_not_retained}

Three further families of variants were evaluated by the same rule and none improves its control (\cref{tab:mi_not_retained_quality}). The reparameterizations of the sensitivity function, against the corrected model: \variant{m-reparam} and \variant{m-asymptote} regress, and \variant{m-constant} gains in \BenchmarkOne and \BenchmarkTwo at the cost of five of the remaining six datasets, each variant adding one or two hyperparameters to a search with a fixed budget of trials. A covariance matrix in place of the scalar variance of a microcluster, with the Mahalanobis distance of \cref{info:mahalanobis_as_teda_metric} and the intersection condition generalized either along the line that joins the two means, \variant{covariance, directional link}, or by the Bhattacharyya distance, \variant{covariance, Bhattacharyya link}, against \MicroTEDAClusPlus: both regress, in \BenchmarkTwo, and neither gains in any dataset, not even in the elongated static shapes. The two order-independent densities of \cref{sec:mi_corrections}, again against \MicroTEDAClusPlus, in which the density only decides which microclusters are active: \variant{peak-density} loses in \ChameleonDSThree and \variant{volume-density} in five datasets. We retain the original function $m$, the scalar variance and the density of the last absorbed sample.

\section{\MicroTEDAClusPlus}
\label{sec:mi_final_model}

\MicroTEDAClusPlus keeps the structure of \MicroTEDAClus: microclusters updated recursively with the TEDA statistics, macroclusters as connected components of the graph of intersecting active microclusters, and pruning of the inactive microclusters by vitality. It differs from the algorithm of the reference implementation in the five points that the experiments accepted, listed in \cref{tab:mi_final_differences}. It has three hyperparameters: the relative variance limit $r$, the decay, with $\rho = 1/\text{decay}$, and the noise distance $n_\sigma$.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption[Differences between \MicroTEDAClusPlus and \MicroTEDAClus]{Differences between \MicroTEDAClusPlus and the algorithm of the reference implementation of \MicroTEDAClus.}
\begin{tabular}{l p{0.36\textwidth} p{0.36\textwidth}}
\toprule
 & \MicroTEDAClus & \MicroTEDAClusPlus \\
\midrule
Membership & macrocluster with the largest density-weighted sum of $1 - \zeta$ & macrocluster of the active microcluster with the smallest standardized distance \\
Update & every microcluster that accepts the sample & the nearest microcluster that accepts the sample \\
Noise & every sample is assigned to a macrocluster & a sample farther than $n_\sigma$ standard deviations from every active microcluster is noise \\
Variance limit & absolute, $r_0$ & relative to the variance of the stream, $r_0 = r\,\sigma^2_{\text{stream}}$ \\
Distances & scaled by $2/d$ & not scaled \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\label{tab:mi_final_differences}
\end{table}

\subsection{Microclusters}

A microcluster $i$ is described by its number of samples $S^i$, its mean $\mu^i$, its variance $(\sigma^i)^2$, its density $D^i$, its vitality $v^i$ and a flag that tells whether it is active. The model also keeps the variance of the stream, $\sigma^2_{\text{stream}}$, the mean squared distance of the samples seen so far to their mean, updated before each batch is learned. For a sample $x$ and a microcluster $i$,

\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
    S' = S^i + 1, \qquad \mu' = \frac{S' - 1}{S'} \mu^i + \frac{x}{S'}, \qquad \sigma'^2 = \frac{S' - 1}{S'} (\sigma^i)^2 + \frac{\lVert x - \mu' \rVert^2}{S' - 1}, \\
    \xi' = \frac{1}{S'} + \frac{\lVert x - \mu' \rVert^2}{S' \sigma'^2}
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:mi_tentative_update}
\end{equation}

where $\xi' = 1/S'$ when $\sigma'^2 = 0$. The sample is an outlier to the microcluster, $\text{outlier} \in [\text{false}, \text{true}]$, if

\begin{equation}
    \text{outlier} =
    \begin{cases}
        \sigma'^2 > r\,\sigma^2_{\text{stream}}, & S' < 3 \\[4pt]
        \dfrac{\xi'}{2} > \dfrac{m(S')^2 + 1}{2 S'}, & S' \ge 3
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:mi_final_outlier}
\end{equation}

\Cref{alg:mi_final_microclusters} processes a sample.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Microcluster update of \MicroTEDAClusPlus for a sample $x$}
\label{alg:mi_final_microclusters}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $x$, the microclusters, $r$, $\rho$, $\sigma^2_{\text{stream}}$
\State $A \gets$ the microclusters to which $x$ is not an outlier by \cref{eq:mi_final_outlier}
\If{$A = \emptyset$}
    \State create a microcluster with $S = 1$, $\mu = x$, $\sigma^2 = 0$, $D = 0$, $v = 1$, flagged as active
\Else
    \State $i \gets \arg\min_{l \in A} \lVert x - \mu^l \rVert$
    \State $v^i \gets v^i + \rho\,(\sigma^i - \lVert x - \mu^i \rVert)/\sigma^i$ if $\sigma^i > 0$, otherwise $v^i \gets 1$
    \State $S^i \gets S'$, $\mu^i \gets \mu'$, $(\sigma^i)^2 \gets \sigma'^2$, $D^i \gets 2/\xi'$, from \cref{eq:mi_tentative_update}
\EndIf
\For{each microcluster $l$ that is not active}
    \State $v^l \gets v^l - \rho$
    \State remove $l$ if $v^l < 0$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Macroclusters}

The macroclusters are rebuilt after each batch. Two microclusters intersect when \cref{eq:microcluster_intersection_condition} holds between their means and standard deviations, which defines the graph $\mathbb{G}_0$. In each connected component of $\mathbb{G}_0$, a microcluster is active if it has at least three samples and its density is not below the mean density of the microclusters of the component. The macroclusters are the connected components of $\mathbb{G}_1$, the subgraph of $\mathbb{G}_0$ induced by the active microclusters.

\subsection{Prediction}

For $q^l(x) = \lVert x - \mu^l \rVert^2 / (\sigma^l)^2$, the standardized squared distance of a sample to an active microcluster $l$, and $l^* = \arg\min_l q^l(x)$,

\begin{equation}
    l_x =
    \begin{cases}
        -1, & q^{l^*}(x) > n_\sigma^2 \\[2pt]
        l_\text{macro}(l^*), & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:mi_final_prediction}
\end{equation}

Before the first batch is learned there is no active microcluster, and every sample is labeled as noise.

\subsection{What Remains of the Original Model}

The function $m$, the density, the activation criterion, the two-pass construction of the macroclusters and the vitality mechanism are those of the reference implementation. We challenged each of them in this chapter with at least one alternative and none of the alternatives improved the model. In particular, the density of a microcluster is still the density of the last sample it absorbed, the order dependence identified in \cref{subsubsec:order_dependent_characteristics}: in \MicroTEDAClusPlus it only decides which microclusters are active, and \cref{subsec:mi_not_retained} verifies whether an order-independent density can replace it there.

We search the hyperparameters in $r \in [10^{-6}, 1]$ and $\text{decay} \in [10, 10^4]$, both on a logarithmic scale, and $n_\sigma \in [1, 10]$.

In the experiments of this chapter, we execute \MicroTEDAClusPlus as a configuration of the implementation of \cref{sec:mi_optimization}. For the main experiment, we implement the model on its own, with its three hyperparameters and without the options of the experimental implementation, and we verified that the two implementations produce the same predictions and the same number of microclusters, batch by batch, on scalability streams with 2, 8 and 32 dimensions.

\section{\MicroTEDAClusPlus in the Main Experiment}
\label{sec:mi_main_experiment}

The experiments above compare models that share one implementation, on runs reserved for the development of the model. In this section, we return to the main experiment of \cref{chap:methodology_reassessment}. We tune and evaluate \MicroTEDAClusPlus on its 94 runs, six datasets and seven scalability streams, by the same code and on the same machine as the benchmark models, and compare it with \MicroTEDAClus and the six benchmark models as \cref{sec:reassessment_results} compares \MicroTEDAClus. The streams of these runs took no part in the development of the model, except those of \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, which are the same in every run (\cref{subsec:mi_runs}).

\subsection{Clustering Quality}
\label{subsec:mi_main_quality}

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/comparison/quality.tex}

In the Overall ARI, \MicroTEDAClusPlus is better than \MicroTEDAClus in five datasets and indistinguishable from it in \Cassini, and in the Steady-State ARI it is better in all six (\cref{tab:mi_comparison_quality}). Against the benchmark models, it reverses the position of \MicroTEDAClus in the streams with concept drift. Its Overall ARI is better than that of every other model in \BenchmarkOne and \BenchmarkTwo, and than all but \TEDACloud in \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, and no model is better than it at the penultimate batch of these streams. In the static shapes, it is better than \MicroTEDAClus, \DenStream and the TEDA-based models, but worse than \CluStream and \StreamKM in \Cassini and \ChameleonDSThree, on both metrics, and worse than \CluStream in the Overall ARI of \ChameleonDSFour.

In \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, \ChameleonDSThree and \ChameleonDSFour, the comparison with \MicroTEDAClus also carries a difference of search budget, since the reference implementation exhausts the time of the search before its trials in these datasets (\cref{tab:mi_optimization_trials}). The validation of \cref{sec:mi_validation}, in which every model completes its trials, shows that the gains over the \variant{vectorized} model do not depend on that difference.

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/comparison/scalability.tex}

In the scalability streams, \MicroTEDAClusPlus is better than every other model from two dimensions on, on both metrics, except \CEDAS, which reaches the same median of $1.000$ and is indistinguishable from it from four or eight dimensions on (\cref{tab:mi_comparison_scalability}). The collapse of \MicroTEDAClus at the end of the streams of high dimension does not occur. The stream with one dimension is the exception: there, \MicroTEDAClusPlus is indistinguishable from \MicroTEDAClus, \CluStream and \StreamKM, with a lower median than \MicroTEDAClus. No one-dimensional stream took part in the development of the model.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{subsec:mi_main_cost}

\input{ModelImprovement/artifacts/comparison/cost.tex}

\MicroTEDAClusPlus is faster than every other model in both stages of every dataset, except in the learning stage of \BenchmarkOne and \RandomRBFGeneratorEvents, where it is indistinguishable from \CEDAS (\cref{tab:mi_comparison_cost}). Most of its prediction times print as zero because they are at or below the 10 ms resolution of the analysis. Its learning time is one to two orders of magnitude below that of \MicroTEDAClus in four datasets and close to three in the Chameleon datasets, which combines the optimization of \cref{sec:mi_optimization} with the number of microclusters that each tuned model keeps, the driver of the cost (\cref{subsec:mi_validation_cost}). As in \cref{sec:reassessment_results}, these comparisons measure implementations as much as algorithms: \MicroTEDAClusPlus runs the sequential part of the algorithm in compiled code, whose contribution \cref{subsec:mi_optimization_time} measures.

Its memory footprint is smaller than those of \MicroTEDAClus and \CluStream in every dataset and larger than those of \CEDAS and \TEDACloud in every dataset. The model stores the microclusters in arrays that always have room for one new microcluster per sample of the next batch and that are enlarged to twice the required size when that room runs out, so the footprint we measure is that of the allocated capacity, the same for every run that never keeps more than 100 microclusters.

\subsection{Summary}
\label{subsec:mi_main_summary}

In the main experiment, \MicroTEDAClusPlus improves on \MicroTEDAClus and changes its position among the benchmark models. It is better than \MicroTEDAClus in the Overall ARI of five of the six datasets and in the Steady-State ARI of all six. No benchmark model is better than it in the streams with concept drift, where \MicroTEDAClus was worse than most of them, and in the scalability streams it is better than every model but \CEDAS from two dimensions on, without the collapse of \MicroTEDAClus. It is the fastest model in both stages of every dataset, tied with \CEDAS in the learning stage of two, and its memory footprint is smaller than those of \MicroTEDAClus and \CluStream and larger than those of \CEDAS and \TEDACloud. The weakness that remains is the clustering of static shapes, where \CluStream and \StreamKM remain better.
